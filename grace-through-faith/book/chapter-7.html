Chapter Seven

Working From Grace Through Delay, Setbacks and Uncertainty

There are seasons when the hardest part of faith is not beginning.

It is continuing.

You have prayed. You have believed. You have prepared. You have worked. You have learned. You have adjusted. You have tried to act responsibly. You have not simply sat down waiting for something to happen.

And yet the outcome you expected has not appeared.

The job has not come. The business has not recovered. The opportunity remains closed. The relationship has not improved. Recovery is taking longer than expected. Something you thought would have changed by now has not changed.

Then another struggle begins.

How long do I keep doing this? Did I misunderstand God?

Does this delay mean I should stop expecting?

If grace is supplied towards the outcome, why have I not seen the outcome yet?

At this point, the greatest difficulty may no longer be the delay itself. It may be what we begin to believe the delay means.

A rejection happens, and we interpret it.

A setback occurs, and we explain it.

Time passes, and we give the passing of time a voice.

Before long, the circumstance is no longer merely telling us what has happened. We have allowed it to tell us what God is doing, what the future holds, and sometimes even who we are.

This is why working from grace through delay requires more than patience. It requires us to understand how to interpret what has not yet happened.

Delay Does Not Explain Itself

Suppose someone has been searching for profitable employment. They have prepared a good CV, developed useful skills, submitted applications and attended interviews. They have prayed and continued to expect God's help.

Then another rejection arrives.

The rejection is real.

But notice what happens next.

The rejection says:

This particular application did not produce the job.

Fear may say:

Nobody will employ me.

Condemnation may say:

Something must be wrong with me.

Performance thinking may say:

Perhaps I have not prayed enough, believed enough or worked hard enough for God to help me.

Despair may say:

Nothing is going to change.

Only the first statement came directly from the event. Everything after it is an interpretation.

This distinction matters.

Delay tells us that the outcome has not appeared yet. By itself, delay does not tell us why.

A setback tells us that something has happened within the process. It does not automatically tell us what God has decided about the outcome.

This does not mean setbacks tell us nothing. They can contain valuable information. A rejected application may reveal that a CV needs improvement. A failed strategy may expose a weakness. A closed opportunity may require us to consider another route.

But information and interpretation are not the same thing.

The believer must learn to receive information from circumstances without allowing circumstances to become a new revelation about God.

That requires us to return to the premise from which faith began.

What Has Actually Changed?

Faith did not begin because circumstances guaranteed an outcome. Faith began because grace gave faith something to believe.

Sometimes that premise is a specific promise from God. But as we have already seen, God's Word reveals much more than individual promises. It reveals God Himself—His character, goodness, mercy, faithfulness, power and willingness to help.

So when circumstances change, an important question is:

Has this circumstance changed what I know about God, or has it changed what I presently understand about the process?

These are very different things.

Perhaps the method has changed.

Perhaps the timing is different from what I assumed.

Perhaps one channel has closed.

Perhaps another person's decision has affected the process.

Perhaps my strategy needs improvement.

Perhaps I simply do not yet understand what is happening.

But none of those things automatically means God's character has changed. The business may be struggling, but Chapter Five has already taught us that the business is not the Source.

A person may respond differently from what we hoped, but Chapter Six has already taught us that our faithful participation does not give us sovereignty over another person's will.

A method may fail without grace failing.

A timetable may stretch without God's character changing.

Where our knowledge of the process ends, our knowledge of God does not have to end with it.

This is especially important because uncertainty creates room for fear to speak.

When Fear Begins Interpreting the Future

Fear often begins with something real.

The money really is low.

The application really was rejected.

The opportunity really did close.

The recovery really is taking longer.

Then a thought appears: What if this never changes?

The thought becomes an interpretation:

My future is closing.

And the interpretation produces anxiety.

This is why anxiety cannot always be addressed simply by telling ourselves to stop feeling anxious. Nor is the answer to repeat words mechanically until uncomfortable emotions disappear.

As we established earlier, the entrance of God's Word gives light.

Light allows us to examine what fear is saying.

What exactly am I afraid this circumstance means?

Is that meaning established by God's Word?

Has God ceased to be good?

Has He ceased to be faithful?

Has He ceased to be my Source?

Has this setback exhausted His ability to help?

Faith does not require us to deny emotional disturbance.

It does something more important:

Faith refuses to give fear exclusive authority to interpret the future.

The Word brings another reality into view.

And once our attention is no longer completely captured by what appears to be wrong, something else becomes possible even before the situation changes.

We can give thanks.

Thanksgiving Before the Outcome

Thanksgiving is easy to understand when something good has just happened.

The job comes.

The provision arrives.

The answer becomes visible.

The recovery is complete.

Then we say, "Thank You, Lord."

And rightly so. But if thanksgiving depends entirely upon favourable circumstances, what happens while the matter remains unresolved?

Paul writes:

"In every thing give thanks."

1 Thessalonians 5:18

This does not require us to call every painful circumstance good.

We do not need to celebrate loss because it is loss.

We do not have to pretend disappointment feels pleasant.

We do not give thanks because sickness, rejection or difficulty is enjoyable.

We give thanks in difficult circumstances because difficult circumstances have not removed God from our lives.

This makes thanksgiving a function of knowledge.

Thanksgiving is not first a response to favourable circumstances. It is a response to what we know about God within our circumstances.

I may still be waiting, and God is with me. I may not understand the process, and God remains faithful.

A channel may have closed, and God remains my Source.

I may not know what to do next, and I can still ask Him for wisdom.

The outcome may not yet be visible, and grace has not disappeared.

Fear says, Look at what is missing.

Thanksgiving does not answer, Nothing is missing.

It says:

What is missing is not all that is true.

Paul makes this especially clear in Philippians 4:6 when prayer, requests and thanksgiving appear together.

That means I can still have a request and still have thanksgiving.

I can want something to change and still give thanks.

I can ask God for what I need without behaving as though His goodness will only become real after I receive it.

Jesus gives us a striking picture of this at Lazarus's tomb. Before Lazarus came out, Jesus thanked the Father that He had heard Him. The visible situation had not yet changed.

The thanksgiving did not deny the tomb. Neither was it a technique for manipulating God. It expressed confidence in the Father before the visible outcome appeared.

That distinction is essential.

We do not give thanks to persuade God to become faithful. We give thanks because we have come to know that He is faithful.

Thanksgiving is therefore not another work through which we earn an outcome.

We do not thank God enough so that He will finally reward our faith.

We do not use thanksgiving to prove that we believe.

Thanksgiving does not create God's presence in difficulty.

It recognises the God who is already present.

And this recognition becomes especially important when time itself begins to argue against our expectation.

When Time Appears to Contradict Faith

Abraham knew this tension. God had spoken concerning a son, but fulfilment did not arrive immediately. Time passed, and the visible circumstances increasingly appeared to argue against what Abraham expected.

Romans 4 does not present Abraham's faith as confidence in favourable circumstances. His persuasion became centred upon God—the One who had spoken and who was able to perform what He had promised.

This teaches us an important distinction.

Where God has given a specific promise, we must distinguish between what God actually said and what we assumed about how and when it would happen.

God's Word is not necessarily the same as our timetable.

His promise is not necessarily our preferred process.

The premise of faith must therefore remain stronger than our assumptions about the process.

Otherwise, when our timetable fails, we may mistakenly conclude that God's faithfulness has failed.

And waiting can create another danger.

When we can no longer control timing, we may begin trying to control outcomes. Abraham's involvement with Hagar shows the danger of attempting to bring about by human arrangement what God had promised to accomplish according to His purpose.

This reveals something important about anxiety:

Anxiety does not always make us passive. Sometimes anxiety makes us excessively active.

We rush.

We manipulate.

We compromise.

We force conversations.

We make financial decisions simply because doing something feels safer than uncertainty.

We become desperate to create movement.

Activity itself is therefore not proof that we are working from faith.

The question remains:

Am I participating because grace is supplied towards the outcome, or am I trying to manufacture the outcome because I am afraid grace will not be enough?

This question becomes even more important when delay tempts us back into performance.

Delay Does Not Change the Order of Grace

When something takes longer than expected, a believer may begin thinking:

Perhaps I need to pray more so that God will finally act.

Perhaps I need to confess more so that I can prove I really believe.

Perhaps I need to work harder so that I can qualify for the result.

Perhaps I need to give something so that God will respond.

Without realising it, we have rebuilt the very system grace delivered us from.

The order has changed from:

Grace→Faith→Expectation→Participation

to:

Performance→Qualification→Grace

But delay does not change the order of grace.

If grace was God's initiative before the delay, it does not become our wage because time has passed.

This does not mean our participation never changes. We may need to pray for wisdom. We may need to work differently. We may need to learn, adjust, apologise, develop another skill or abandon an ineffective method.

But these are acts of participation and stewardship.

They are not payments made to persuade God to become gracious.

We do not begin working for grace simply because fulfilment has taken longer than we expected.

Sometimes, in fact, the process may look nothing like the outcome we expected.

Joseph shows us how to live there.

When the Process Looks Wrong

Joseph received dreams that pointed towards a future he could not yet see.

What followed did not immediately look like progress towards that future.

He was sold.

He became a servant.

He was falsely accused.

He was imprisoned.

If every present circumstance had been allowed to interpret the whole story, there would have been many opportunities to conclude that everything had gone wrong.

Yet something important continued.

Joseph kept carrying the responsibilities of the season he was actually in.

He served in Potiphar's house.

He accepted responsibility there.

He served in prison. He used what was available to him in the place where he actually was.

This connects directly to what we established about assignment.

When the larger outcome is unclear, the present assignment can still be clear.

You may not know when the job will come, but you may know what today's preparation requires.

You may not know how the business will recover, but you may know which weakness needs attention.

You may not know how a relationship will eventually develop, but you may know what faithful conduct requires from you today.

You may not know when full physical strength will return, but you may know what today's responsible care requires.

We do not need complete knowledge of the entire process in order to recognise the responsibility immediately before us.

This gives waiting a different meaning.

Waiting Can Contain Work

Waiting describes a condition.

Something expected has not yet happened. But the word waiting does not automatically tell us what to do during that period.

Sometimes waiting contains preparation.

Sometimes it contains learning.

Sometimes it contains work.

Sometimes rebuilding.

Sometimes another assignment.

Sometimes treatment.

Sometimes rest.

And sometimes there may genuinely be nothing further to do about that particular matter today.

This protects us from two opposite mistakes.

One is passivity:

I am waiting on God, therefore I should do nothing.

The other is frantic activity:

If I stop doing things, the outcome will never happen.

Grace asks a better question:

What does faithfulness require from me while this remains unresolved?

For the person searching for employment, perhaps today requires reviewing the CV.

After several rejections, the person notices that applications rarely lead to interviews.

That is information.

They review the CV and discover that it does not clearly communicate their strongest skills.

So they improve it.

That is learning.

They recognise weakness in interviews and practise.

They develop another useful skill.

They continue applying.

Perhaps they accept responsible temporary work while continuing the search.

None of these actions means the earlier expectation has been abandoned. They show that participation can change as information increases.

This is how grace teaches us to handle setbacks.

A Setback Is Information Before It Is Meaning

When we act, we receive feedback.

Sometimes the result is encouraging.

Sometimes it is not.

A strategy fails.

An application is rejected.

A business decision produces poor results.

A plan needs revision.

The performance mindset often makes evaluation threatening because failure of the method feels like failure of the person.

Grace separates the two.

A failed method does not have to become a failed identity.

Because my standing with God is not being determined by whether every strategy succeeds, I can look honestly at what happened.

I can say:

I was wrong.

This method is not working.

I need help.

I need more information.

I need another strategy.

That honesty is not the enemy of faith.

It can be the fruit of security.

Faith does not require emotional loyalty to an ineffective method.

Expectation can remain steady while methods remain flexible.

Changing the CV is not abandoning the expectation of employment.

Changing a business model is not necessarily abandoning the assignment. Changing the way we communicate does not mean we have abandoned the relationship.

Seeking another channel does not mean we no longer believe God is the Source.

We evaluate.

We learn.

We adjust.

Then we participate again.

But sometimes even after evaluation, we still do not know what to do.

Grace has room for that too.

When We Do Not Know What to Do

There are moments when no obvious strategy presents itself.

Jehoshaphat expressed this honestly:

"We do not know what to do, but our eyes are on you."

2 Chronicles 20:12

That is not a confession of unbelief. It is an admission of human limitation combined with a decision concerning where attention will remain.

There is great freedom in being able to say:

I do not know.

Faith does not require us to invent certainty God has not given.

We may not know the next method.

We may not know the timing.

We may not know which channel will open.

We may not understand why the process has taken this form.

But:

I may not know what to do next and still know where to look.

So we pray.

We search God's Word.

We seek wisdom.

We receive appropriate counsel.

We remain attentive to the Spirit's leading. We give thanks for God's presence.

And where there is no further responsible action to take today, we do not have to manufacture one simply to escape the discomfort of uncertainty.

Tomorrow may reveal what today does not.

Until then, grace remains available today.

Continuing the Grace Cycle

Delay does not require another spiritual system.

The same pattern remains:

Receive→Believe→Expect→Act→Evaluate→Learn→Return to Grace→Act Again

We receive by returning to what grace has revealed and what God has supplied for the present.

We believe by remaining persuaded about God's character and what He has actually revealed rather than allowing circumstances to rewrite Him. We expect by continuing to look forward without making our assumptions about timing and method equal to God's Word.

We act where the present assignment requires participation.

Then we evaluate.

What happened?

What worked?

What did not?

What information has this setback given us?

We learn.

Perhaps the method needs to change.

Perhaps our understanding needs to grow.

Perhaps another channel should be explored.

Perhaps the wisest action is to wait.

And when fear, condemnation or performance thinking returns, we return to grace.

We remember the premise.

Then we act again.

Sometimes this cycle moves quickly. Sometimes we pass through parts of it repeatedly before the expected outcome becomes visible.

Delay does not break the Grace Cycle.

It teaches us how deeply we have learned to live from it.

And throughout that cycle, thanksgiving keeps reminding us that the unresolved outcome is not the whole story.

I Still Have Reason to Give Thanks

Perhaps the job has still not come.

The believer can continue applying, learning and improving while saying:

Thank You, Father, because You have not left me.

Perhaps the business has not fully recovered.

The owner can continue stewarding, adjusting and working while giving thanks for wisdom, present provision and God's continuing presence.

Perhaps recovery is gradual.

The believer can receive appropriate care, steward today's strength and still thank God. Thanksgiving does not cancel the request.

It keeps the request from becoming the only truth in our consciousness.

I can still have a request and still have reason to give thanks.

This is one of the ways faith survives long seasons.

It remembers that we know more about God than we know about the process.

The resurrection itself stands at the centre of Christian faith as a reminder that present appearance does not possess final interpretive authority.

At the cross, what observers could see looked like defeat.

But what they could see was not enough information with which to interpret the whole work of God.

The resurrection does not give us permission to promise that every failed project will return, every lost opportunity will reopen, or every situation will end exactly as we imagined.

It teaches something deeper:

Present circumstances do not know enough to become our final revelation of God.

God has revealed Himself.

So when the process is incomplete, we do not have to complete the story with fear.

Continuing From Grace

Perseverance is not merely doing the same thing for a very long time.

Sometimes perseverance means continuing.

Sometimes it means changing the method.

Sometimes learning.

Sometimes admitting a mistake.

Sometimes taking another route.

Sometimes waiting.

Sometimes resting.

Sometimes giving thanks while the request remains unanswered.

What makes all these expressions perseverance is the foundation from which they come.

We are still working from grace. Delay has not changed the order.

Setbacks have not made us rejected.

Uncertainty has not made God absent.

A constrained channel has not constrained the Source.

A failed method has not become our identity.

A longer process has not rewritten God's character.

And so we continue.

I will not allow delay to rewrite what grace has taught me about God. I will not turn a setback into condemnation, a failed method into a verdict on the future, or uncertainty into evidence that God has left me. I will distinguish what God has revealed from what I assumed about timing, process and method. Where there is something to do, I will participate. Where there is something to learn, I will learn. Where a strategy must change, I will change it. Where wisdom requires waiting, I will wait without trying to manufacture what only God can supply. And while I wait, I will give thanks—not because every circumstance is pleasant, but because no circumstance has removed God from my life. I do not give thanks to persuade Him to become faithful. I give thanks because I know that He is. I may not yet understand the process, but I still know God. Grace remains supplied towards the outcome, and from that foundation I will continue.
