Preface

Why This Book

There is a kind of exhaustion that can exist even in a sincere life of faith.

You believe in God. You believe in Jesus Christ. You pray. You serve. You give. You try to obey God's Word. You have been taught to trust Him and believe His promises. Yet somewhere along the journey, believing God can begin to feel like another job you must perform successfully.

You need something from God, so you pray. But after praying, another question arises: Have I prayed enough?

You believe His promise, but difficulty remains: Am I believing strongly enough?

A fearful thought enters your mind: Have I just cancelled my faith?

Someone else testifies about an answer to prayer while you are still waiting: Why did it happen for them and not for me? A business struggles. A plan fails. An opportunity disappears. A prayer appears unanswered. Suddenly, the questions become more personal:

What have I done wrong?

Is God displeased with me?

Do I need to do something more before God will act?

And so we work harder.

We pray harder. We try to believe harder. We confess more carefully. We give. We serve. We attempt to remove every trace of uncertainty from our minds.

Without realising it, something has changed.

Faith, which should rest upon the faithfulness of God, has become another performance through which we try to qualify for His goodness.

This raises one of the central questions behind this book:

❦

What if we have rejected salvation by works but recreated the psychology of works inside our life of faith?

The Problem Is Not Work

This book is not an argument against work.

The Bible could hardly be used to make such an argument.

Scripture repeatedly calls people to obedience, diligence, service, perseverance, stewardship and action. The apostle Paul, one of the New Testament's clearest teachers of grace, was also a man who spoke repeatedly about labour.

His words in 1 Corinthians 15:10 contain what initially appears to be a contradiction. Paul attributes everything he is to the grace of God, yet in the same verse he speaks about labouring abundantly.

Grace and labour exist in the same sentence.

That observation raises an important question.

If grace means that God's gift cannot be earned through human performance, why labour?

And perhaps an even more important question is:

From what foundation should we labour?

There is a difference between working for grace and working from grace.

That difference lies at the heart of this book.

What Do We Mean by the Psychology of Grace?

The word psychology in the title does not mean that this book is intended to be a clinical psychology textbook.

We are concerned with something more basic and deeply practical: the beliefs, expectations and interpretations that influence the way we behave.

What do I believe about God while I am working?

What do I believe about myself when I fail?

What happens inside me when another person's life appears to be progressing while mine seems to have stopped?

How do I interpret delay?

How do I interpret difficulty?

Why can uncertainty make me afraid to begin?

What happens to my willingness to attempt something difficult when failure no longer determines my worth before God?

And what happens to perseverance when my expectation rests upon God's faithfulness rather than my ability to control the process? These are psychological questions, but the answers this book seeks will begin with Scripture.

Psychological observations may help us describe human behaviour. They cannot replace the authority of God's Word.

Working for Grace and Working From Grace

Working for grace begins with uncertainty about God's willingness.

It says:

"If I perform correctly, believe sufficiently and do everything expected of me, perhaps God will give me what I need."

Working from grace begins somewhere very different.

It begins with God.

It asks:

"What has God provided? What has He promised? What has He revealed about Himself? What has He made available in Christ?"

Faith then becomes our response to God's revelation rather than our attempt to manufacture God's response.

This does not make us passive.

It may actually give us a greater reason to work. If grace has provided, faith can receive.

If faith receives, expectation can arise.

And if we genuinely expect, we have a reason to prepare, act, learn, build and persevere.

The question is no longer simply:

How much am I doing for God?

There is another question underneath it:

❦

What do I believe about God while I am doing it?

That is the journey I invite you to take through this book.
