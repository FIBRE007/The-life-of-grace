Chapter Three

Grace at Work: Labouring Because God Is at Work

If grace has changed what we believe, what should happen to the way we work?

This question is important because grace is sometimes misunderstood as the opposite of effort. If God has provided, why labour? If we believe God for an outcome, why apply ourselves strongly towards it?

At the other extreme is the believer who works as though everything depends on human effort. Such a person may pray and speak about trusting God, but the real pressure of producing the outcome rests entirely on their shoulders.

Neither position properly describes working from grace.

Grace does not make labour unnecessary. Neither does labour make grace unnecessary.

Paul gives us one of the clearest pictures of their relationship:

"But by the grace of God I am what I am: and his grace which was bestowed upon me was not in vain; but I laboured more abundantly than they all: yet not I, but the grace of God which was with me."

1 Corinthians 15:10

Paul says two things that may appear contradictory.

"I laboured."

Then:

"Yet not I."

He does not deny his effort. He says he laboured more abundantly. He applied himself.

Yet when Paul explains his labour, he refuses to present himself as its independent source.

Grace was with him.

This gives us an important foundation:

Grace does not remove effort. Grace changes the reason for effort, supplies us in our effort, and changes how we understand the results of our effort.

Working from grace means that we apply ourselves because we believe God is at work.

Grace Gives Faith a Reason to Expect

In the previous chapter, we saw that faith is not something we manufacture to persuade God to become gracious.

Grace comes first.

God reveals Himself through His Word. We discover His character, faithfulness, power and provision. That knowledge gives faith something to stand on.

The heart becomes persuaded.

And persuasion creates expectation.

If I believe God is faithful, I begin to interpret my future differently.

If I believe God supplies, present lack does not have to become my final prediction.

If I believe God gives wisdom, not knowing what to do today does not mean I can never know what to do.

Faith therefore affects what I expect.

But expectation was never intended to replace participation. It gives participation a reason.

Consider a farmer who has absolutely no expectation of a harvest. Planting would appear pointless. But because he believes a harvest is possible, he prepares the ground and plants.

In the same way, grace gives the believer a reason to look forward.

Faith receives that revelation and says:

"Because of what I know about God, I can expect."

Then labour says:

"Because of what I know about God, I can apply myself."

This gives us a useful order:

Grace revealed→Faith→Expectation→Labour→Outcome→Glory to God

But grace does not disappear after the first step.

Grace remains the supply throughout.

Because God Works, We Work

Jesus gives us an important picture in John 5. After healing a man at the pool of Bethesda, Jesus said:

"My Father worketh hitherto, and I work."

John 5:17

He later explained that the Son does what He sees the Father doing.

The Father is not "grace"; He is a Person. But His activity shows us something important about grace-centred work.

Jesus' activity was connected to the Father's activity.

The pattern was not:

I work so that My Father will become involved.

It was:

My Father is working, therefore I work.

This difference is at the centre of this book.

Working for grace says:

"I must work so that God will help me."

Working from grace says:

"Because God is involved, I can work." One makes human effort the attempt to produce divine involvement.

The other makes divine involvement the reason for human participation.

This changes the psychology of work.

The believer no longer approaches responsibility thinking:

Everything depends on me.

Instead:

God is at work. Grace is available. Therefore, I can participate fully.

Labour Is Participation

This gives us a better way to understand Christian labour.

Labour is participation.

When we work from grace, we apply our minds, strength, skills, time and available resources towards an outcome because we believe God is not absent from the process.

This does not make our effort imaginary.

Paul really laboured. The student really studies.

The parent really raises the child.

The unemployed person really searches for work.

The business owner really serves customers.

The writer really writes.

The farmer really plants.

The believer really participates.

Grace does not say, "You did nothing."

Grace says:

"You were never doing it alone."

This is why effort and earning must be separated.

Effort says:

"I will apply myself."

Earning says:

"Because I have applied myself, God owes me the outcome."

Grace rejects earning, not effort. Philippians 2:12–13 expresses this relationship beautifully. Paul tells believers to work out what God is working in them, because it is God who works in them both to will and to do according to His good pleasure.

Again, divine activity does not produce passivity.

God works.

Therefore, we work.

Faith Without Labour, Labour Without Faith

This helps us understand James' statement that faith without works is dead.

Faith that never affects our participation is incomplete in its expression.

Suppose someone believes God for profitable employment.

They declare:

"I believe God will provide a good job."

But they refuse to prepare a CV, develop useful skills, search for opportunities, submit applications or prepare for interviews.

That is not working from grace.

Expectation should influence participation. But there is another person.

This person sends applications constantly, studies, develops skills and attends interviews, but inwardly believes:

There are no opportunities for me.

Everything depends on my ability.

If this interview fails, my future is finished.

If I cannot make this happen, nobody can help me.

Their hands are working, but their heart is carrying the entire future.

That is also not working from grace.

One person has expectation without meaningful participation.

The other has participation without grace-centred expectation.

To labour with the wrong mindset can be as futile as having the right mindset without applying ourselves.

Grace brings both into their proper relationship.

Faith gives labour expectation.

Labour gives expectation participation. And grace supplies both.

We Plant, We Water, God Gives the Increase

Paul gives us another important picture in 1 Corinthians 3.

Speaking about ministry, he says:

"I have planted, Apollos watered; but God gave the increase."

Paul planted.

Apollos watered.

God gave the increase.

None of those statements cancels the others.

Paul did not say, "Since God gives increase, planting is unnecessary."

Neither did he say, "Because I planted, I created the increase."

This is the balance of grace-centred labour.

We plant.

We water.

God gives the increase. Our participation matters, but we are not the Source.

This distinction protects us from both passivity and pride.

Passivity says:

"If God gives increase, I need not plant."

Pride says:

"Because I planted, the increase came from me."

Grace says:

"I will plant faithfully because I trust the God who gives increase."

Grace Changes the Quality of Our Work

If labour is participation with grace, careless work cannot be our ideal.

Grace should make us willing to learn.

To prepare.

To practise.

To improve.

To plan. To ask questions.

To develop skill.

To receive correction.

To evaluate our methods.

Colossians 3:23 says:

"And whatsoever ye do, do it heartily, as to the Lord, and not unto men."

Grace does not lower the quality of our work.

It changes the reason we pursue quality.

Performance says:

"I must be excellent because failure will prove that I am inadequate."

Grace says:

"I can pursue excellence because this work matters, I have received capacity from God, and the fruit of my work can glorify Him."

One produces effort driven by fear.

The other allows effort to grow from confidence, responsibility and gratitude. This also means grace gives us freedom to admit when our work needs improvement.

A Failed Method Is Not Necessarily a Failed Promise

Suppose a believer starts a small business.

They have reason to expect that the business can become profitable. They pray, plan, invest available resources, develop a product and begin selling.

But sales remain poor.

Performance thinking may say:

"I have worked hard. Why hasn't God rewarded me?"

Passivity may say:

"God told me this will work, so I should just keep doing exactly what I am doing."

Grace-centred labour asks better questions.

Is the product actually solving the customer's problem?

Am I reaching the right people?

Is my pricing sensible? What are customers saying?

What do I need to learn?

What part of my strategy needs to change?

The person may discover that the product is good but the marketing is poor.

Changing the marketing method is not unbelief.

It may be wisdom.

Grace allows us to distinguish between an expected outcome and the method we currently use to pursue it.

A failed method does not automatically mean the expected outcome has failed.

This is why grace-centred labour must include evaluation.

Correction Without Condemnation

Performance makes evaluation difficult because every mistake feels personal.

Instead of saying:

"This method did not work,"

we say: "I am a failure."

Instead of:

"I need to learn something,"

we conclude:

"Perhaps I am not good enough."

Grace gives us enough security to tell the truth about our work.

We can admit:

I prepared poorly.

I misunderstood the market.

I need more skill.

I wasted resources.

My approach needs to change.

without turning those statements into:

God has rejected me.

Correction becomes information rather than condemnation.

That makes improvement possible.

Perseverance Is More Than Repetition

This also changes how we understand perseverance.

Perseverance does not mean repeating an ineffective method forever simply because changing it feels like unbelief.

Faith can remain steady while strategy changes.

Grace-centred perseverance combines:

Conviction + Learning + Adaptation + Continued Participation

The believer remains persuaded about God while remaining teachable about methods.

This is important because expectation should make us resilient, not rigid.

If one approach fails, we learn.

If we discover a weakness, we strengthen it.

If circumstances change, we adjust.

If we need help, we ask.

If we need more knowledge, we learn.

Then we continue. The expected result keeps us looking forward, while grace frees us to learn along the way.

The Grace Cycle

This gives us a practical pattern for working from grace:

Receive→Believe→Expect→Act→Evaluate→Learn→Return to Grace→Act Again

Receive.

Recognise what God has revealed and what He has already supplied.

Believe.

Allow what you know about God to become the premise for faith.

Expect.

Look forward with confidence rather than allowing present limitations to define the entire future.

Act.

Apply yourself. Plan. Prepare. Learn. Work. Participate. Evaluate.

Look honestly at what your labour is producing.

Learn.

Allow success, difficulty, feedback and even failed methods to teach you.

Return to Grace.

When something goes wrong, do not immediately return to condemnation, fear or self-dependence. Remember the Source.

Act Again.

Adjust what needs adjustment and continue.

Grace is not only present at the beginning of this cycle.

Grace surrounds the entire process.

Grace gives faith its premise.

Grace gives expectation confidence.

Grace supplies labour.

Grace allows honest evaluation.

Grace removes condemnation from learning.

Grace gives strength to begin again.

Why God Gets the Glory

When the outcome finally appears, what should the believer say?

Paul gives us the answer.

"I laboured."

There is no need to pretend we did nothing.

You studied.

You planned.

You practised.

You worked.

You persisted.

You changed strategies.

You developed skill.

You participated.

But Paul adds:

"Yet not I, but the grace of God which was with me."

Behind our participation were realities we did not create.

Life. Time.

Strength.

Wisdom.

Capacity.

Opportunity.

Relationships.

Resources.

The ability to learn.

And ultimately, God who gives the increase.

This is why the result should produce glory rather than pride.

Grace does not require us to deny our labour.

Grace teaches us to interpret our labour correctly.

We worked, but we were never the Source.

We planted.

We watered.

God gave the increase. This is what it means to work from grace.

Not:

"I will work until God becomes involved."

But:

"Because God is involved, I will work."

Not:

"Everything depends on me."

But:

"My participation matters, but I am not the Source."

Not:

"If I believe, I do not need to labour."

But:

"Because I believe, I have a reason to participate."

Grace gives faith something to believe.

Faith creates expectation. Expectation gives us reason to move forward.

We apply ourselves because we believe God is at work.

And when fruit appears, we recognise the Source.

I do not labour to convince God to work. I labour because I believe God is at work. I will prepare, learn, plan, practise, plant, water, evaluate, adjust and continue. I will apply myself fully without carrying the burden of becoming the Source. My labour is participation with grace. I labour, yet not I alone, but the grace of God with me. And when the increase comes, the glory belongs to God.

The question now becomes more practical.

If the believer is called to labour from grace, what exactly are we meant to give our lives and labour to?

That question takes us into purpose, assignment, work and calling.
