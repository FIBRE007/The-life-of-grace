Chapter Two

Grace Through Faith: When the Heart Becomes Persuaded

One of the easiest ways to misunderstand faith is to turn faith itself into work.

A believer faces a difficult situation and begins to ask:

Do I have enough faith?

Have I prayed enough?

What if I have doubted?

Have I confessed the right Scriptures enough times?

What if something I said has affected the outcome?

Soon, the believer is no longer mainly looking at God. He is examining himself. His confidence rises when he thinks he has performed well and falls when he notices fear, doubt or weakness.

Faith has become another requirement to satisfy. This is the same performance thinking that grace came to free us from, only now it is wearing religious clothes.

Paul writes:

"For by grace are ye saved through faith…"

Ephesians 2:8

The order matters.

Grace comes first. Faith receives.

We see this clearly in eternal life. Romans 6:23 calls eternal life "the gift of God." God does not owe us eternal life as wages for believing correctly. Life is God's gift to us in Christ.

Faith does not create the gift. Faith receives it.

This gives us a foundation for understanding the relationship between grace and faith throughout the Christian life:

Faith does not persuade God to provide. Faith responds to what grace reveals about God and what He has provided.

But this raises an important question.

If faith receives, what gives faith confidence to receive?

The God Behind the Promise

Romans 4 gives us an important answer.

God had made Abraham a promise. Yet when Paul describes Abraham's faith, he tells us something deeper than the fact that Abraham remembered what God said.

Abraham became "fully persuaded that, what he had promised, he was able also to perform" (Romans 4:21).

Abraham had a promise, but his confidence rested in the God who made the promise.

This distinction matters.

A promise is only dependable when the person making it is dependable. God's promises are trustworthy because God is trustworthy.

So the believer needs more than knowledge of God's promises. We need knowledge of God Himself.

Scripture does not only tell us what God has promised. It reveals the One who promises.

It shows us His faithfulness.

His mercy.

His wisdom. His compassion.

His power.

His goodness.

His consistency.

This means we should not study the Bible only asking:

"What can I believe God for?"

We should also ask:

"What does this reveal to me about the God I am believing?"

That question gives faith deeper roots.

A promise tells me something God has said. His character tells me why I can trust the One who said it.

When God Gives a Specific Word

2 Chronicles 20 shows what happens when God gives people a specific word concerning their situation.

King Jehoshaphat received news that a large army was coming against Judah. He was afraid. More importantly, he understood that Judah did not have the strength to solve the problem by itself.

His prayer included a remarkable confession:

"Neither know we what to do: but our eyes are upon thee."

2 Chronicles 20:12

They recognised their inability and turned towards God.

Then God spoke through Jahaziel. He told them not to be afraid because the battle was God's.

Later Jehoshaphat told the people:

"Believe in the LORD your God, so shall ye be established; believe his prophets, so shall ye prosper."

2 Chronicles 20:20

Their faith had a clear premise. God had revealed what He intended to do.

But notice the order again.

Their believing did not force God to help them.

God revealed His provision, and His Word gave them something to believe. Grace was not absent during the period of the law. Here we see human weakness meeting divine help, followed by a call to believe what God had revealed.

Sometimes our situation will be like Jehoshaphat's. God's Word gives us a clear promise upon which expectation can rest.

But what happens when we cannot find a promise describing the exact situation we face?

Does faith disappear because we cannot say, "God specifically promised me that this particular event would happen in this particular way"?

No.

Because God's Word gives faith something deeper than individual promises.

It gives us God.

When There Is No Specific Promise

Look at the people who came to Jesus.

In Matthew 8, a leper approached Him and said:

"Lord, if thou wilt, thou canst make me clean."

There is no recorded personal promise given to this man beforehand saying that Jesus would heal him.

Yet he came.

Why?

Because he already believed something about Jesus.

"Thou canst."

He was confident in Christ's ability. His uncertainty was about Christ's willingness.

Jesus answered that uncertainty:

"I will; be thou clean."

The encounter revealed more than Jesus' power. It revealed something about His disposition towards the man who came to Him.

Later in the same chapter, a Roman centurion approached Jesus concerning his sick servant. He was so persuaded about Christ's authority that he believed Jesus did not need to enter his house. Christ's word was enough.

The Canaanite woman in Matthew 15 came from another position. She appealed to Jesus for mercy concerning her daughter and continued looking to Him for help. These people did not all approach Jesus holding the same promise.

But they were responding to something they believed about the same Person.

One recognised His ability.

Another recognised His authority.

Another appealed to His mercy.

Their confidence helps us understand something important:

Faith does not always require a promise describing every detail of the outcome. But faith always requires a premise—and God's revealed character provides a premise for trusting Him.

This does not make God's promises less important. It helps us understand why His promises are powerful.

Behind every promise stands the Promiser.

God's Word therefore does more than give us statements to claim. It introduces us to the God we trust.

And nowhere is God revealed more clearly to us than in Christ. Jesus does not merely tell us things about God. His life reveals the Father's heart. His compassion, holiness, authority, mercy and faithfulness show us what God is like.

Hebrews 13:8 tells us:

"Jesus Christ the same yesterday, and to day, and for ever."

That does not mean every situation will unfold in exactly the same way. God's methods may differ. The process may surprise us. Timing may differ from what we expected.

But the character of the One we trust is not constantly changing.

This becomes extremely important when circumstances seem to tell us otherwise.

When the Storm Starts Telling the Story

Mark 4 records Jesus crossing the sea with His disciples.

Before they left, Jesus said:

"Let us pass over unto the other side."

Then a violent storm arose.

Waves entered the boat. The boat began filling with water. And Jesus was asleep.

The danger was real.

Faith does not require us to pretend that the storm does not exist.

The disciples could see the waves. They could feel the wind. They could see water entering the boat.

Their problem was deeper.

They allowed the storm to begin telling them what their future would be—and what Jesus' behaviour meant.

They woke Him and said:

"Master, carest thou not that we perish?"

Their words are revealing.

They did not simply say:

"There is a storm."

Their circumstances had produced an interpretation:

"We are going to perish."

And the fact that Jesus was sleeping produced another:

"You do not care." The storm was no longer simply around them. It was beginning to shape what they believed about the Person in the boat with them.

Jesus arose and calmed the storm. Then He asked:

"Why are ye so fearful? how is it that ye have no faith?"

Afterwards, the disciples asked:

"What manner of man is this, that even the wind and the sea obey him?"

That question takes us directly to the real issue.

Who is this?

They had Jesus in the boat, but their knowledge of Him was not yet strong enough to interpret the storm correctly.

Grace was present.

Power was present.

Authority was present.

Help was present.

Yet circumstances had become more persuasive than what they understood about the One who was with them. This is where many battles of faith take place.

A delay happens, and we conclude:

God has forgotten me.

An opportunity closes:

God will not provide.

Something we prayed about gets worse:

Maybe God does not care.

We make a mistake:

Perhaps God will no longer help me.

Circumstances are real, but our interpretation of them is not always true.

The battle of faith is often a battle over which interpretation of reality will have the greatest authority in our hearts.

What Doubt Can Reveal

This helps us understand doubt and anxiety without condemning ourselves for experiencing them. Doubt can grow whenever something becomes more persuasive to us than what God has revealed.

Sometimes it is circumstances.

Sometimes it is delay.

Sometimes it is past disappointment.

And sometimes it is our own performance.

We may believe God is able while also thinking:

But have I prayed enough?

Was my faith strong enough?

Did I confess correctly?

Have I done enough for God to help me?

Our confidence becomes divided.

Part of it rests in God's grace. Another part rests in our ability to perform correctly.

The answer is not to become frightened because doubt has appeared.

Neither is the answer to pretend anxiety does not exist.

We need something stronger than fear. We need light.

When the Word Brings Light

Psalm 119:130 says:

"The entrance of thy words giveth light…"

God's Word does not simply give us religious sentences to repeat.

It helps us see correctly.

Fear may tell us that God has abandoned us. The Word reveals His faithfulness.

Circumstances may make us question whether He cares. The Word reveals His compassion.

Our weakness may make us think everything depends upon us. Grace reveals God's sufficiency.

The Word corrects the conclusions we have drawn about God from our circumstances.

That is why Romans 10:17 says:

"So then faith cometh by hearing, and hearing by the word of God."

Faith does not have to be manufactured by force. The Word enters.

Light comes.

God is revealed.

The heart becomes persuaded.

Faith grows as God's testimony about Himself becomes more persuasive than the testimony of our circumstances.

And when the heart becomes persuaded, something begins to happen to our words.

We Believe, Therefore We Speak

Romans 10:10 says:

"For with the heart man believeth unto righteousness; and with the mouth confession is made unto salvation."

Paul is speaking about salvation, but notice the relationship.

The heart believes.

The mouth confesses.

2 Corinthians 4:13 makes the order even clearer:

"We believe, and therefore speak."

Psalm 19:14 similarly joins "the words of my mouth" with "the meditation of my heart."

Our words matter, but words are fruit before they are technique.

The disciples' words in the storm revealed what had become persuasive inside them. Their words did not create the storm. Their words exposed their interpretation of it.

This is why confession should never become mechanical.

A believer can repeatedly say, "God cares for me," while inwardly being persuaded that God has abandoned him.

The solution is not to become terrified of saying the wrong words.

Neither is it simply to speak louder.

Return to the Word.

See God again.

Allow His Word to challenge what fear has taught you about Him.

Let light enter.

As the heart becomes persuaded, speech begins to change because the source of the speech has changed. Mark 11:23 connects believing in the heart with speaking. The words come from a heart that believes.

Biblical confession is therefore not an attempt to force God to produce an outcome. It is the expression of a heart increasingly persuaded about God.

We do not speak to make God faithful.

We speak because we have become persuaded that He is faithful.

We do not believe in the greatness of our faith.

We believe God.

And when God answers, the testimony should not finally be:

"Look how perfectly I believed."

It should be:

"Look what God has done."

Grace provides.

The Word reveals.

Light enters.

The heart becomes persuaded.

The heart believes. And because we believe, we speak.

I do not need my faith to make God gracious. I do not need to perform faith perfectly before God becomes willing to help me. I give His Word entrance into my heart because His Word reveals who He is. When circumstances speak fear, I return to what God has revealed about Himself. When I do not understand the process, I can still know the Person. As His character becomes clearer to me, my heart becomes persuaded. And because I believe, I speak.

But faith cannot end with what is believed in the heart or spoken with the mouth.

The next question is unavoidable:

What happens when what we believe begins to shape the way we work?
