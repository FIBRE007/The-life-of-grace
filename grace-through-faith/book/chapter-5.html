Chapter Five

Working From Grace in Provision, Money and Resources

An assignment gives labour direction, but it also creates requirements.

Once we recognise what has been entrusted to us, we soon discover that willingness alone is not enough to carry it out. A parent needs resources to care for a family. A student needs time, knowledge, opportunity and strength. A business needs customers, wisdom, skill and money. A person searching for employment needs information, access and opportunity. Decisions require wisdom. Work requires capacity. Life itself requires food, shelter, health and relationships.

So the question that naturally follows Chapter Four is not simply, What has been entrusted to me? It is also:

What should I believe about what is required to carry it out? The question becomes especially important when what we presently have appears smaller than what the assignment requires. It is easy to remain confident when the money is available, the body is strong, the customers are coming and the necessary people are willing to help. But what happens when there appears to be a gap between the outcome before us and the resources presently in our hands?

That gap is where many believers begin to carry a burden that was never theirs to carry. We move from being responsible participants to trying to become the source of everything the future will require.

Grace calls us back to a more fundamental truth:

God is the Source. Resources are channels and tools.

Understanding that distinction changes not only how we think about money, but how we think about work, health, relationships, opportunity, wisdom and the future itself.

The Source and the Channel

A salary is valuable, but a salary is not the Source.

A business can provide income, but the business is not the Source. A customer matters, but the customer is not the Source.

Savings matter, but savings are not the Source.

A relationship may bring help, but that person is not the Source.

A doctor may help preserve or restore health, but the doctor is not the Source of life.

All these things can become channels through which God's supply reaches us. Because they are channels, they should not be despised. A salary should be managed wisely. Customers should be served well. Relationships should be honoured. Savings should be stewarded. Doctors should be listened to appropriately. Opportunities should be taken seriously.

Dependence upon God does not require contempt for the means He uses.

The problem begins when we give the channel the psychological position that belongs to the Source.

If my employer has become my source, the loss of my job does not merely mean that one means of income has closed. It begins to feel as though provision itself has disappeared.

If one major customer has become my source, losing that customer feels like losing my future. If one person has become my source of help, that person's inability to help begins to feel like there is no help anywhere.

This is one reason resource constraints can produce such intense anxiety. The visible channel has become so large in our thinking that we can no longer imagine supply apart from it.

But a channel can be constrained without God being constrained.

A job can end without God ceasing to provide.

A customer can leave without God becoming poor.

A person can say no without heaven becoming empty.

One opportunity can close without exhausting every possibility available to God.

When we confuse the channel with the Source, the limitation of the channel begins to look like the limitation of God.

Working from grace requires us to separate the two.

And once we make that separation, we begin to recognise that God's supply is much broader than the physical resources we normally count.

Sometimes the Supply Is Knowing What to Do

When we think about provision, we naturally think about things: money, food, equipment, customers, accommodation, medicine or other tangible resources.

But sometimes the most important thing required towards an outcome is not another possession. It is wisdom.

You may not need more money first. You may need to know what to do with the money you already have.

A business may not initially need more customers. The owner may first need to understand why existing customers are not returning.

A person looking for employment may need direction towards a skill that should be developed.

A family facing a difficult decision may need wisdom before additional resources would even be useful.

God's supply can therefore include wisdom, understanding, instruction and direction.

We see this principle in Scripture.

At the wedding in Cana, when the wine ran out, Mary told the servants concerning Jesus:

"Whatsoever he saith unto you, do it."

John 2:5

Jesus then gave instructions. The servants filled the waterpots with water and drew from them as He directed. The miracle was God's work, but the servants' participation followed instruction.

Peter experienced something similar in Luke 5. He and the other fishermen had worked through the night and caught nothing. Jesus did not merely announce that fish existed. He gave Peter direction concerning the net.

Peter responded to that word, and his participation was directed towards an outcome he had been unable to produce through his previous effort.

The point is not that every problem will be solved through a dramatic instruction. Neither should every passing thought be labelled the voice of God. Scripture gives us reason for both faith and wisdom. God's leading will not contradict His revealed character and Word, and believers should not abandon sound judgement simply because they feel strongly about an idea.

But we should enlarge our understanding of supply.

Supply is not limited to what God places in our hands. It can include the wisdom and direction by which He shows us what to do with our hands.

Romans 8:14 speaks of the children of God being led by the Spirit of God. That means the believer is not limited to looking anxiously at resources while trying to calculate the entire future alone. We can pray for wisdom. We can search the Scriptures. We can remain attentive to the Spirit's leading. And when direction becomes clear, we can respond responsibly.

Sometimes that direction leads us towards another channel.

An idea may lead to an opportunity.

An instruction may lead to a conversation.

A conversation may lead to a relationship.

A relationship may provide information.

Information may change a decision.

A changed decision may open a route we could not previously see.

The Source was never trapped inside the first channel. This becomes especially important when that first channel becomes constrained.

Constraint Does Not Mean the Source Has Failed

Suppose a believer is building a small business while supporting a family. For some time, one customer has provided a significant part of the business's income. Then the customer leaves.

The loss is real.

Faith does not require the business owner to pretend otherwise. Revenue has fallen. Expenses remain. Responsibilities at home have not disappeared.

But what does the loss mean?

Fear may immediately interpret the event:

Everything is over.

Performance thinking may say:

I must somehow manufacture enough money to guarantee that nothing goes wrong.

Grace allows a different question:

What exactly has become constrained? God—or one customer?

Supply itself—or one channel through which supply had been coming?

The distinction does not immediately put money into the bank. What it does is protect the person's mind from interpreting a present constraint as a final verdict.

Constraint is information about the present condition; it is not necessarily a verdict concerning God's capacity to supply towards the outcome.

That calmer position makes responsible thought possible.

The business owner can review expenses. They can protect essential family needs. They can examine savings. They can contact other customers. They can study why the business became too dependent on one client. They can improve the offer. They can pray for wisdom and remain attentive to direction.

This is not unbelief.

It is stewardship.

And stewardship becomes much easier when we stop demanding that today's inventory guarantee the whole future.

Present Inventory Is Not the Measure of the Future

One of the psychological effects of fear is that it turns an inventory into a prophecy.

We count what we have today and unconsciously conclude:

This is all that can ever be available.

If today's money appears insufficient, tomorrow becomes impossible.

If today's opportunities are few, the future becomes closed.

If today's strength is low, future capacity is defined by present weakness.

But grace teaches us to look honestly at what is presently available without making it the final measure of what can become available.

Present supply is real, but it is not necessarily the full measure of future supply.

Jesus' teaching in Matthew 6 repeatedly directs attention towards the Father's knowledge and care concerning daily needs. In the prayer He taught His disciples, we find the simple request:

"Give us this day our daily bread."

Matthew 6:11

Daily bread is an interesting picture because it does not require that a lifetime of bread be visible before today's confidence becomes possible.

Israel experienced this very practically in the wilderness.

God supplied manna.

But the people still gathered it.

Dependence did not eliminate participation.

At the same time, they were not required to manufacture tomorrow's manna today. There was supply for the day and responsibility within the day.

Daily provision taught dependence without eliminating daily labour.

This gives the believer a healthier way to approach the future:

I can be fully responsible for today's stewardship without assuming responsibility for becoming tomorrow's Source.

That does not mean refusing to plan for tomorrow. Scripture does not condemn wise preparation. It means recognising the difference between planning for the future and trying to guarantee the future through present control.

We can save without making savings our god.

We can plan without believing our plan is the source.

We can prepare without demanding complete visibility.

We can take today's step without possessing everything the entire journey will eventually require.

God's supply may be progressive.

Today's wisdom may come before tomorrow's wisdom.

One opportunity may come before the next.

One relationship may precede another.

One customer may come before a larger market.

One instruction may reveal the next step without revealing the entire route.

Forward-looking faith therefore does not say, I can see everything.

It says, I do not need to see everything before I faithfully respond to what is before me.

Confidence in the Supplier

Paul gives us a mature picture of this confidence in Philippians 4.

Before saying, "My God shall supply all your need according to his riches in glory by Christ Jesus" in verse 19, Paul speaks about his own experience of changing circumstances.

He knew what it was to have little.

He knew what it was to abound.

He had experienced fullness and hunger.

His confidence in God therefore did not depend upon never encountering constraint.

That matters.

If Philippians 4:19 is interpreted to mean that a believer will never experience a season in which visible resources appear limited, Paul's own words in the same chapter challenge that interpretation.

The stronger truth is that constraint does not have to destroy confidence in the Supplier.

The believer's confidence is not ultimately measured by the size of the present channel but by the sufficiency of the Source.

That changes the question from: How large is what I presently possess?

to:

What has God supplied, what is it for, and how should I steward it?

And that final question is important because supply has purpose.

Provision Is Purposeful

2 Corinthians 9 gives us a useful picture of God's supply. In verses 8–11, God's abundance and sufficiency are connected with good works and generosity.

The movement is significant.

God supplies.

Sufficiency is created.

Capacity for good work increases.

Generosity becomes possible.

Supply is therefore not presented merely as accumulation.

Provision is purposeful.

This takes us directly back to assignment. Money matters because there are responsibilities to meet.

Wisdom matters because decisions must be made.

Strength matters because there is life to live and work to do.

Relationships matter because very little meaningful work is completely solitary.

Opportunity matters because action needs a context.

Health matters because bodily capacity affects our participation in life.

The mature question concerning a resource is therefore not merely:

How much do I have?

It is:

What is this for?

Paul develops this idea further in 2 Corinthians 9:10 when he speaks about God supplying "seed to the sower" and "bread for your food."

Bread and seed are both supply, but they do not have the same immediate purpose.

Bread is consumed. Seed is sown.

If everything that should become seed is continually consumed as bread, future fruitfulness can be damaged.

The principle reaches beyond money.

Some income may be needed for present expenses, while another portion can be saved or invested.

Time has moments for work and moments for rest.

Strength must sometimes be spent and sometimes restored.

Knowledge can solve an immediate problem and can also be developed into greater capacity.

Recognising supply is therefore only the beginning of stewardship. Wisdom asks what that supply is meant to accomplish.

And that brings responsibility back to us.

We Steward; We Do Not Become the Source

God's place and our place should not be confused.

God remains Source.

We receive, recognise, steward and participate. We work.

We plan.

We learn.

We manage.

We save.

We invest wisely.

We ask.

We seek.

We knock.

We develop capacity.

We follow sound direction.

We use what is available.

And we remain attentive to the assignment.

This is why confidence in God's supply should never become an excuse for carelessness.

"I trust God" is not a reason to waste money.

It is not a reason to refuse to budget. It is not a reason to avoid developing useful skills.

It is not a reason to ignore information.

It is not a reason to refuse work.

Faith does not say:

God provides, therefore stewardship does not matter.

It says:

Because God is the Supplier, I can steward what He supplies without fear becoming my master.

I can budget without believing the budget is my source.

I can save without making the savings my security.

I can work without making the job my provider.

I can use a channel without worshipping the channel.

The same principle applies to health.

Stewarding the Capacity to Live and Work

Health belongs inside the supply argument because our bodies are part of the capacity through which we participate in life and assignment. When health is challenged, we should not suddenly abandon everything we have learned about Source and channel.

A doctor may become a channel.

Treatment may become a channel.

Medication may become a channel.

Rest may become a channel.

Nutrition may become a channel.

Wisdom may direct us towards one of these channels.

The Spirit's leading may impress upon a believer the need to stop, seek help, speak to someone, change an unhealthy pattern or take another responsible step.

Using such a channel does not mean God has ceased to be the Source of life.

A believer can gratefully use the channel without confusing the channel with the Source.

Nor should present health constraint automatically become the definition of the future.

If capacity is reduced today, wisdom may require reduced workload today. That is not necessarily unbelief.

The believer can pray, seek appropriate care, rest, receive help and steward present strength while continuing to expect increased capacity.

We can steward today's strength while expecting tomorrow's supply.

This does not condemn the person whose recovery is gradual. Faith is not measured by pretending that pain, weakness or limitation does not exist. Grace allows us to acknowledge the present condition while refusing to make that condition our only premise concerning the future.

Our business owner, for example, may now face two constraints at once. The major customer has left, and temporary ill health has reduced the amount of work that can be done.

Fear sees the shrinking channels and announces that everything is collapsing.

Grace asks what faithful participation looks like now.

Perhaps the person seeks appropriate care, reduces non-essential work, reviews expenses, uses savings responsibly, accepts temporary help, contacts other customers and acts upon new wisdom concerning the business. The channels may now look very different from those available a month earlier.

But this is precisely the point:

The present channel has changed. My Source has not.

And Scripture repeatedly shows us that the Source is not trapped inside one method.

The Source Is Not Trapped Inside One Channel

Elijah received food through ravens in one season and later experienced provision in the home of a widow.

Israel received manna in the wilderness.

Joseph's wisdom and administration became part of God's means of preserving many lives during famine.

Paul sometimes received material support from believers and at other times worked with his own hands.

The lesson is not that we should expect God to reproduce these exact methods. It is that we should not imprison God's supply inside the method presently familiar to us.

A familiar channel can be honoured without becoming an idol.

And this freedom also changes the way we hold what has already been supplied.

Fear of future lack can cause a person to move beyond prudent preparation into hoarding. The heart begins to say:

I cannot release anything because I may never receive again.

Savings themselves are not the problem. Wise reserves can be good stewardship. The deeper question is where confidence rests.

2 Corinthians 9 connects God's supply with generosity because people who know that resources are not the Source can hold those resources differently.

Giving does not purchase God's willingness to supply.

We do not give in order to make God become Provider.

Giving does not make God a Supplier. Giving becomes possible because we believe He already is one.

That keeps generosity inside grace rather than turning it into another performance system. And by this point, the pattern of grace-centred stewardship should be familiar.

Receiving and Stewarding Supply

We receive by recognising what God has already supplied.

What money is available?

What strength?

What wisdom?

What relationships?

What tools?

What opportunities?

What information?

What direction?

We believe by remaining persuaded that God is the Source rather than transferring our confidence to what is presently visible.

We expect by looking forward concerning what is still required without demanding that the entire future become visible before we move.

Then we act. We work with what is available. We budget. We plan. We seek wisdom. We follow sound direction. We develop capacity. We seek appropriate care. We rest when necessary. We save. We serve. We give.

As we participate, we evaluate.

Which channels are working?

Which are becoming constrained?

Where is waste occurring?

What has changed?

What does the assignment require next?

Evaluation allows us to learn. Perhaps our stewardship needs to improve. Perhaps another skill is needed. Perhaps an old channel has become too important in our thinking. Perhaps an instruction we have received requires action.

And when constraint tempts us to return to fear, we return to grace.

We remember the Source.

Then we act again.

This is the same grace cycle:

Receive→Believe→Expect→Act→Evaluate→Learn→Return to Grace→Act Again

But grace is not merely the first stage of the cycle. Grace remains God's supply towards the outcome throughout it.

That is why we can continue.

What Does the Assignment Require Next?

In the end, provision must return us to purpose.

Resources are not the destination.

Life has something to do.

Health matters because there is life to live.

Money matters because there are responsibilities to meet.

Wisdom matters because decisions must be made.

Strength matters because there is labour.

Relationships matter because we live and work among people. Direction matters because sometimes the difference between remaining stuck and moving forward is knowing what faithful participation requires next.

So perhaps the mature question is not simply:

How much do I have?

It is:

How faithfully am I using what God has presently supplied towards the assignment before me?

And then:

What does the assignment require next?

Sometimes the answer may be money.

Sometimes knowledge.

Sometimes an opportunity.

Sometimes a conversation.

Sometimes an instruction.

Sometimes another person.

Sometimes treatment. Sometimes rest.

Sometimes preparation.

And sometimes the next requirement is simply faithful use of what is already in our hands.

Working from grace means that we do not make ourselves the source of any of these things.

Neither do we sit passively waiting for them.

We receive.

We believe.

We expect.

We participate.

I am not the source of my life, health, wisdom, strength or provision. God is. I do not need to possess everything the future will require before I faithfully participate today. I will recognise what God has already supplied. I will remain attentive to His wisdom and direction. I will honour the people, opportunities and resources He uses without making any of them my source. I will work, plan, learn, save, seek, ask, rest, receive help and act responsibly. If one channel becomes constrained, I will not conclude that the Source has become constrained. Present inventory will not become the prophecy of my future. Grace is supplied towards the outcome, and my responsibility is faithful participation with what is presently available while continuing to expect God concerning what is still required. Resources may change. Channels may change. Seasons may change. But God remains the Source.
