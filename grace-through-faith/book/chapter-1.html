Chapter One

The Performance Problem: When Righteousness Becomes Something We Try to Earn

From an early age, we learn that performance produces results.

A pupil studies for an examination and receives a grade. An employee completes a month's work and receives a salary. A salesperson closes a deal and earns a commission. An athlete trains, competes and hopes that good performance will lead to selection or victory. A business that consistently creates value expects customers to pay for that value.

There is nothing inherently wrong with this arrangement. Much of society could not function without some relationship between performance and consequence.

The problem begins when we take a principle designed for certain human relationships and assume that it explains the foundation of our relationship with God. Without realising it, we can construct a spiritual equation:

Performance→Righteousness→God's Approval→Blessing

If I perform well, I expect God to respond favourably. If things go well, I interpret them as evidence that God is pleased with me. When things go badly, I search my life for the performance failure that caused God to withhold His goodness.

The result is much bigger than a theological misunderstanding.

It changes the way we think.

It changes the way we work.

It changes the way we interpret success.

It changes the way we respond to setbacks.

And eventually, it can even change what we mean when we say that we have faith.

When Performance Becomes Identity

Performance itself is not the enemy.

There is a difference between saying, "I did this poorly," and saying, "I am worthless because I did this poorly." The first statement evaluates an action. The second uses the action to establish identity.

This distinction matters because human beings can easily move through a cycle:

Performance→Evaluation→Identity→Emotional Response→Further Performance

We perform an action and evaluate the result.

If it succeeds, we may feel capable, valuable and confident. If it fails, we may feel embarrassed or disappointed.

But when performance becomes the foundation of identity, something deeper happens. Success no longer simply means, "That worked." It begins to mean, "I am somebody because that worked."

Failure no longer means, "That did not work." It begins to mean, "There is something wrong with me."

This creates two possible problems.

Success can produce pride.

Failure can produce condemnation.

And both can produce fear. The successful person becomes afraid of losing the identity that achievement has created. The unsuccessful person becomes desperate to achieve something that will repair a damaged sense of worth.

So we work again.

Not merely because the work needs to be done, but because we need the result to tell us something about ourselves.

This becomes even more serious when we bring the same psychology into our relationship with God.

When Circumstances Become God's Report Card

Suppose I believe that righteous performance produces God's favour.

When my business succeeds, I may conclude that God is pleased with me.

When my family prospers, I may see it as evidence that I have lived correctly.

When an opportunity appears, I may interpret it as proof of God's approval.

But what happens when the business struggles? What happens when the opportunity disappears?

What happens when someone who has prayed faithfully experiences a difficult season?

The same system now works in reverse.

If blessing proves righteousness, perhaps hardship proves unrighteousness.

If success proves God's approval, perhaps difficulty proves His displeasure.

Life itself becomes a spiritual report card.

This way of thinking is not new.

The book of Job confronts it with remarkable force.

Job and the Problem With the Equation

Job's story begins with a description that immediately creates difficulty for a simplistic performance-and-reward theology.

Job is presented as a man of remarkable integrity who fears God and turns away from evil (Job 1:1). Yet devastating circumstances enter his life.

Job's friends attempt to make sense of what has happened. Their reasoning often assumes a predictable moral equation: God is just, therefore severe suffering must indicate serious wrongdoing.

Eliphaz asks Job to consider when the innocent have perished and where the upright have been destroyed (Job 4:7). Bildad similarly argues from God's justice (Job 8). Their arguments contain statements that can sound reasonable when separated from Job's actual situation.

That is what makes their error important.

They are not simply saying foolish things.

They are applying an inadequate framework to a complicated reality.

They look at Job's circumstances and reason backwards.

Suffering has occurred. Therefore: serious failure must have occurred.

But the reader has information Job's friends do not possess. The opening chapters have already challenged their explanation.

Their mistake teaches us something important:

❦

Circumstances cannot always function as a reliable measurement of someone's standing with God.

Performance religion does more than evaluate behaviour. It encourages us to interpret circumstances as verdicts.

A person loses an opportunity and immediately asks, "What did I do wrong?"

Someone else receives an opportunity and we assume, "They must be doing something right."

A Christian experiences financial difficulty and wonders whether God is punishing them.

Another Christian prospers and begins to believe prosperity proves superior spirituality.

Job's friends show us how dangerous this reasoning can become.

We can be deeply convinced that we are defending God's justice while completely misunderstanding what is happening in another person's life.

The Pharisee Who Had Evidence

Jesus exposes another side of the performance problem in Luke 18. Luke tells us that Jesus addressed the parable to people who trusted in themselves that they were righteous and treated others with contempt (Luke 18:9).

A Pharisee and a tax collector go to the temple to pray.

The Pharisee has evidence to present.

He is not describing laziness or open rebellion. He speaks of fasting and giving. These are practices that, in themselves, are not wrong.

The problem is what his performance has come to mean.

His confidence before God has become inseparable from his achievements.

And almost immediately, performance produces comparison.

He is thankful that he is not like other people—including the tax collector standing nearby.

This is one of the psychological consequences of performancebased righteousness.

If my performance establishes my righteousness, I need a way of measuring that performance.

Other people become convenient measuring instruments. Someone who appears to be doing worse makes me feel successful.

Someone who appears to be doing better can make me feel threatened.

The tax collector brings something very different.

He does not present a résumé.

He appeals for mercy.

Jesus concludes that it is the tax collector who goes home justified rather than the Pharisee (Luke 18:14).

The problem was not that fasting was wrong.

The problem was not that giving was wrong.

The problem was confidence in performance as the basis of righteousness before God.

A good action had been given a job it could not perform.

Paul's Impressive Résumé

Few people understood this problem more personally than the apostle Paul.

In Philippians 3, Paul lists the credentials he once possessed. If anyone had grounds for confidence in the flesh, Paul could present an impressive case. He speaks of his heritage, his relationship to the law, his zeal and the righteousness associated with his former religious life (Philippians 3:4–6).

These were not insignificant achievements within the system in which Paul lived.

Yet something happened to Paul's accounting system.

The things he once regarded as gain were re-evaluated in the light of Christ.

Paul's great discovery was not that discipline had suddenly become evil or that obedience no longer mattered. The same Paul would later speak repeatedly about discipline, obedience, perseverance and labour.

What changed was the location of his confidence.

He no longer wanted a righteousness constructed from his own record. He desired the righteousness that comes through faith in Christ (Philippians 3:9).

This gives us an essential distinction:

❦

Something can be valuable as obedience while being useless as currency for purchasing righteousness.

Prayer can be valuable.

Giving can be valuable.

Service can be valuable.

Discipline can be valuable.

Hard work can be valuable.

But once we turn these things into currency with which we attempt to purchase God's acceptance, we have assigned them a role Scripture does not give them.

Paul did not stop working when he discovered grace.

That will become important later.

What changed first was the foundation upon which he stood.

The Collapse of Boasting

Romans 3 takes the argument deeper. Paul's conclusion concerning humanity leaves little room for constructing righteousness from human achievement. After developing the problem of sin, he turns towards a righteousness from God that is manifested apart from the works of the law and received through faith in Jesus Christ (Romans 3:21–26).

Then Paul asks a revealing question:

Where is boasting?

It is excluded (Romans 3:27).

Why does that matter?

Because boasting is a natural consequence of a system in which superior performance establishes superior standing.

If I have achieved righteousness through something I accomplished better than you, I possess grounds for saying, "Look what I did."

Grace destroys that ground for boasting.

Galatians 2:16 reinforces the point by rejecting justification through works of the law and directing confidence towards faith in Christ.

But here we encounter a subtler problem. Christians can agree with Paul that righteousness is not earned through works and still recreate the same performance system somewhere else.

We can replace works with faith performance.

When Faith Becomes Another Performance

Consider the difference.

The old religious equation says:

Good Works→Righteousness→Approval→Blessing

We reject it.

Then, almost without noticing, we construct another:

Strong Enough Faith→God's Approval→Blessing

The vocabulary has changed.

The psychology has not.

Now the believer begins asking:

Have I prayed long enough? Have I confessed correctly?

Did I give enough?

Did I believe strongly enough?

Did that moment of fear cancel my faith?

Did I say something negative that stopped God from acting?

Do I need to demonstrate greater faith before God will respond?

Prayer is biblical.

Giving is biblical.

Confession is biblical.

Perseverance is biblical.

Faith itself is indispensable.

The question is not whether these things have a place in Christian living.

The question is what we believe they are accomplishing.

Are they responses to the grace and revelation of God?

Or have they become payments through which we attempt to purchase His response?

There is a profound difference between the two. If I believe God's willingness to act ultimately depends upon the quality of my faith performance, my attention inevitably turns inward.

Instead of asking, "Is God faithful?"

I continually ask, "Is my faith good enough?"

Instead of confidence being anchored primarily in the object of faith, I become preoccupied with evaluating the quality of my believing.

Faith becomes psychologically exhausting when its object quietly shifts from God's sufficiency to our ability to believe sufficiently.

Peter, the Wind and the Hand of Jesus

Peter's experience in Matthew 14 provides an interesting picture.

The disciples see Jesus walking on the water. Peter responds, asking Jesus to command him to come.

Jesus says, "Come" (Matthew 14:29).

Peter steps out of the boat.

His action has a foundation: the word of Christ.

But as Peter becomes overwhelmed by the wind, fear takes over and he begins to sink. He cries out to Jesus for rescue. There is something important here that can easily be missed if we read the story only through performance.

Peter's faith becomes weak.

Yet Jesus does not stand at a distance waiting for Peter to manufacture better faith before He becomes willing to help.

Peter cries out, and Jesus reaches for him.

Jesus does address Peter's doubt, but Peter's moment of weakness does not reveal a Christ unwilling to respond until Peter has repaired himself.

This does not make faith irrelevant.

It begins to show us that grace may be more fundamental to the relationship than our ability to perform faith perfectly.

We will return to that idea.

When Failure Becomes Too Dangerous

Now bring this into everyday work.

Imagine a Christian starting a business.

The business matters deeply to them. They have prayed about it. They believe God is involved in their journey.

Then the first strategy fails. If they understand failure simply as information, they can ask useful questions.

Was the product right?

Was the pricing wrong?

Did we misunderstand the customer?

Do I need better skills?

Was the timing poor?

What can I change?

But suppose the failed strategy means something much larger:

"This proves I did not have enough faith."

Now evaluation becomes frightening.

Admitting that the strategy was wrong may feel like admitting that faith was wrong.

The person may keep repeating an ineffective method because changing it feels spiritually dangerous.

Or they may abandon the entire goal because one failed attempt has become a verdict.

The same can happen to a student who performs poorly, a leader whose project struggles, or someone pursuing a difficult career. When every outcome becomes a verdict on identity and faith, experimentation becomes dangerous.

Yet much of meaningful human work requires learning.

We attempt.

We observe.

We correct.

We develop competence.

We sometimes discover that what looked sensible in theory does not work in practice.

This pattern can be seen throughout ordinary human history. Scientific discovery, engineering, medicine, enterprise and technological development have repeatedly advanced through observation, testing and correction. Progress often requires people to distinguish between "this approach did not work" and "there is no point continuing."

A failed attempt and a failed identity are not the same thing.

Performance-based spirituality makes that distinction harder to maintain.

Success Has Its Own Danger

The performance system does not only wound people who struggle.

It can deceive people who succeed.

Suppose a Christian prays for a business opportunity and receives one.

The business grows.

Income increases.

Doors open.

If the person believes these outcomes are payment for superior faith, gratitude can quietly become self-congratulation.

"I knew how to believe."

Then they meet another believer whose business is struggling.

The temptation is to reason like Job's friends:

"They must be doing something wrong."

Perhaps they are not praying correctly.

Perhaps they have doubt.

Perhaps they have not given enough. Perhaps they do not understand faith.

The vocabulary is Christian, but the logic is ancient.

We have returned to the Pharisee.

"I am not like them."

One person is condemned by performance.

The other is made proud by it.

Both are trapped.

The Trap of Comparison

This also explains why comparison becomes so powerful.

If blessings are payment for spiritual performance, then another person's circumstances appear to reveal their spiritual ranking.

Their success becomes information about me.

Their opportunity makes me question mine.

Their testimony makes my waiting feel like rejection.

Their visible progress becomes evidence that perhaps God prefers them. Instead of receiving another person's testimony as evidence of God's goodness, I experience it as evidence of my deficiency.

The performance system turns brothers and sisters into competitors.

And underneath the competition lies the same question:

What must I do to become worthy of what they received?

But what if that is the wrong question?

A Different Foundation Must Exist

Job warns us against turning circumstances into automatic verdicts.

The Pharisee shows us that good religious behaviour can become the foundation of self-righteousness.

Paul shows us that an impressive spiritual résumé cannot provide the righteousness we need before God.

Romans 3 removes boasting by locating righteousness somewhere other than superior human performance.

And our own Christian experience reveals something even more subtle: we can take faith itself and turn it into another performance. We can say that salvation is by grace while spending our lives desperately trying to demonstrate that we are finally worthy of grace.

Perhaps the deepest problem with performance religion is not simply that it makes us work too hard.

It makes us work from the wrong foundation.

We attempt to establish through performance what only God can give.

We try to earn what, once earned, could no longer properly be called grace.

And if we transform faith into the payment God requires before He becomes gracious, we may have changed our vocabulary without escaping the old system.

So the most important question is no longer:

How can I improve my spiritual performance enough to qualify?

A different question is beginning to emerge:

❦

If I cannot earn my standing before God, what has God already provided—and how do I receive it?

To answer that, we must move from performance to grace.

And we must discover what Scripture actually means when it says that what God gives is received through faith.
