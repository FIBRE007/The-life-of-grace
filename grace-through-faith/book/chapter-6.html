Chapter Six

Working From Grace in Relationships, Family and Service

Grace changes the way we believe.

It changes the way we work.

It changes the way we understand provision.

And because so much of life is lived with other people, grace must also change the way we relate to them.

This may be one of the places where working from grace is most difficult, because relationships involve something our work with money, time and other resources does not: another human will.

Money can be budgeted. Time can be scheduled. A tool can be used. A business process can be improved. But people think, choose, respond and sometimes refuse to respond in the way we expect. Yet many of life's assignments are relational. We are parents, children, spouses, siblings, friends, neighbours, colleagues, leaders, followers, caregivers and members of communities. These roles require real work. Relationships require listening, communication, patience, correction, forgiveness, sacrifice, honesty, service and responsibility.

So grace does not remove relational labour.

It changes the foundation from which we labour.

And perhaps nowhere is that change more necessary than in our expectations of what our labour should produce in another person.

When Love Becomes a Transaction

There is nothing necessarily wrong with expecting good things from people.

We expect honesty from people we trust.

We expect responsibility from people to whom responsibility has been given.

Parents expect children to learn.

Leaders expect people to fulfil agreed responsibilities.

Friends reasonably expect loyalty. Marriage carries expectations of faithfulness, care and mutual responsibility.

The problem begins when expectation quietly becomes a transaction.

I did this for you, therefore you owe me.

I supported you, therefore you must support me.

I sacrificed for you, therefore you must become what I expect.

I apologised, therefore you must forgive me immediately.

I served faithfully, therefore people must appreciate me.

I raised you properly, therefore you must never disappoint me.

Now something has changed.

Our labour is no longer simply our participation in the relationship. It has become the price we believe should purchase another person's response.

This is the performance system appearing inside relationships.

We perform.

We expect a particular result from another person.

Then we use their response to decide whether our love, sacrifice or service was worthwhile. That is why one of the most painful sentences in relationships is:

After everything I have done for you...

Sometimes that sentence simply expresses genuine hurt. Betrayal hurts. Ingratitude hurts. Dishonesty hurts. Rejection hurts.

Grace does not ask us to pretend otherwise.

But sometimes the sentence reveals something deeper: what we called love had quietly become an investment from which we expected a guaranteed return.

The important question then becomes:

Was my love a gift, or had I quietly turned it into currency?

That question takes us back to the source of Christian love.

We Love Because He First Loved Us

1 John 4:19 gives us a simple but powerful order:

"We love because he first loved us."

We did not first produce enough love to persuade God to love us.

God loved us. We received His love.

And from what we received, we learned to love.

The order is important:

Grace→Reception→Response

This is the same pattern that has been developing throughout this book.

Grace comes first.

Our response follows.

So when grace enters relationships, the other person's response no longer has to become the source from which our capacity to love is continually drawn.

We are not loving merely to obtain love.

We are not serving merely to obtain recognition.

We are not forgiving merely to obtain an apology.

We are not parenting merely to produce children whose choices will prove that we were successful parents.

We are responding to what we ourselves have received from God.

We do not create grace by loving. We love because grace has already met us.

This does not make another person's response unimportant. Healthy relationships require mutual responsibility. But it means their response does not become the payment that gives our obedience its value.

And this is where the principle of Source and channel from the previous chapter becomes important again.

People Are Precious, but They Are Not the Source

God often works through people.

A friend can encourage us.

A spouse can support us.

A parent can provide.

A teacher can guide us.

A leader can open an opportunity.

A colleague can help us solve a problem. A customer can become a channel through which income reaches a business.

People matter enormously.

But just as a job, customer or bank account must not psychologically take the place of God as Source, neither should another person become the source of our identity, worth, peace or security.

When that happens, relationships begin carrying a weight they were never designed to carry.

If people's approval becomes the source of my worth, criticism threatens my identity.

If my child's choices become the source of my identity as a parent, every wrong choice becomes a verdict against me.

If appreciation becomes the source of meaning in service, being overlooked makes service feel worthless.

If another person's affection becomes the source of my security, I may begin trying to control that person so that I can remain secure.

We may call this love.

Sometimes it is fear. And fear often tries to control what grace teaches us to entrust to God.

That brings us to one of the most important distinctions in relationships:

Grace gives us responsibility, but it does not give us sovereignty over another person's heart.

Responsibility Is Not Control

Consider parenting.

A parent has enormous responsibility.

Parents provide.

They teach.

They protect.

They correct.

They encourage.

They listen.

They pray.

They model values. They establish boundaries.

They create routines.

They make sacrifices.

Their participation matters.

A careless parent cannot simply say, "God is in control," as an excuse for refusing responsibility.

Grace does not produce passivity.

But a different problem develops when the parent begins believing:

If I do everything correctly, I can guarantee what my child will eventually become.

Now responsibility has quietly become an attempt at control.

A parent has influence.

A parent has authority.

A parent has responsibility.

But a parent is not sovereign over another human will. The original chapter makes this distinction directly: parents are responsible for faithful parenting, but they cannot control every choice their child will ever make. That does not reduce the importance of parenting.

It actually allows the parent to examine their responsibility more honestly.

Did I listen?

Did I teach?

Did I model what I expected?

Did I correct wisely?

Did I create appropriate boundaries?

Was I present?

Did I make mistakes that I need to acknowledge?

Do I need to apologise?

These questions concern the parent's participation.

They are legitimate.

But another question belongs to a different category:

Can I control my child's heart?

No.

And recognising that limit is not failure. It is humility.

The same principle reaches far beyond parenting.

Influence Is Not Sovereignty

We influence one another constantly.

A good teacher can influence a student.

A leader can influence a team.

A spouse can influence a spouse.

A friend can influence a friend.

Our words matter.

Our examples matter.

Our choices matter.

Our attitudes matter.

Our prayers matter.

Our service matters.

But influence and control are not the same thing.

I can communicate clearly without controlling whether you listen. I can apologise sincerely without controlling whether you immediately forgive me.

I can forgive without controlling whether you change.

I can teach without controlling whether you accept what I teach.

I can advise without controlling whether you follow the advice.

I can lead faithfully without controlling every person's attitude towards my leadership.

I can serve without controlling whether anyone recognises my service.

I can love without controlling whether love is returned in the form I expect.

This is where grace protects us from a terrible burden.

I am responsible for my faithful participation. I am not responsible for becoming sovereign over another person's response.

Once that distinction becomes clear, relationships become a place where faith and labour can operate together without turning labour into manipulation.

I can expect good fruit.

I can pray for change. I can hope for reconciliation.

I can work towards improvement.

But expectation does not give me permission to control another person in order to manufacture the outcome.

That difference is especially important when relationships become difficult.

When Someone Disappoints You

Imagine a parent who has invested deeply in a child for years.

There has been provision.

Teaching.

Prayer.

Correction.

Sacrifice.

Encouragement.

Then the child makes a choice that deeply disappoints the parent.

Pain enters first.

Then performance thinking enters behind it. After everything I did, this should never have happened.

And very quickly, the child's choice becomes the parent's identity:

I have failed.

Grace does not tell the parent not to care.

Neither does grace automatically declare the parent blameless.

Perhaps mistakes were made.

If so, they can be acknowledged.

Perhaps communication was poor.

It can improve.

Perhaps correction was unnecessarily harsh.

That can be addressed.

Perhaps an apology is needed.

It can be given.

Perhaps stronger boundaries are now necessary.

They can be established. Grace makes honest evaluation possible because our identity does not have to be protected by pretending we never did anything wrong.

But grace also protects the parent from assuming responsibility for what never belonged entirely to them.

The child has a will.

The child makes choices.

So the parent can repent where necessary, learn, adjust, pray, continue loving, speak truth and establish appropriate boundaries without accepting the impossible burden of controlling the child's heart.

That is resilient love.

It neither denies responsibility nor assumes sovereignty.

And once we understand this, forgiveness itself becomes clearer.

Forgiveness Does Not Require Pretending

Sometimes Christians struggle with forgiveness because we confuse forgiveness with the removal of every relational consequence.

But these are not identical. Someone can be forgiven and still need to rebuild trust.

A relationship can continue while requiring new boundaries.

Love can remain while access changes.

Mercy can be offered while wisdom recognises a repeated harmful pattern.

Grace does not require us to call dishonesty honesty.

It does not require us to call betrayal faithfulness.

It does not require us to pretend that something did not hurt when it did.

Forgiveness releases our claim to personal vengeance and refuses to make bitterness the governing principle of our response.

But trust concerns confidence in another person's reliability, and confidence may need evidence before it is restored.

Boundaries concern wise decisions about responsibility and access.

So these ideas should not be confused.

Forgiveness can be given while trust is being rebuilt and boundaries are being wisely maintained.

This means grace can produce both tenderness and firmness.

You can forgive and say no.

You can love and create distance where wisdom requires it.

You can wish someone well without allowing the same destructive behaviour to continue without consequence.

You can speak truth without hatred.

You can establish a boundary without revenge.

Grace does not make us weaker in relationships.

It frees us from needing anger, fear or control to make us strong.

And that freedom becomes especially important in service and leadership.

Serving Without Making Recognition the Reward

Service is another place where love can quietly become transactional.

We serve.

We give time.

We carry responsibility. We solve problems.

We help people.

Then nobody notices.

Or somebody else receives the recognition.

Suddenly something rises inside us:

After everything I have done...

Again, the disappointment may be understandable.

Appreciation is good. Honour is good. People should learn to express gratitude.

But if recognition becomes the source of meaning in our service, then the people we serve eventually gain control over our willingness to serve.

Their applause energises us.

Their silence empties us.

Grace offers another foundation.

Our service can have value before God even when human recognition is limited. That does not mean we should remain indefinitely in environments where people exploit us. Wisdom and boundaries still matter.

But we no longer need appreciation to become the payment that proves our service was worthwhile.

The same truth changes leadership.

An insecure leader needs people to respond in certain ways because their response protects the leader's identity.

Disagreement feels like disrespect.

Questions feel like rebellion.

Another person's success feels threatening.

Control becomes attractive because control creates the appearance of certainty.

But a leader established in grace can listen.

They can admit mistakes.

They can correct without humiliation.

They can receive feedback without losing identity.

They can make difficult decisions without needing everybody to approve. They can develop people without fearing their growth.

Grace does not remove authority.

It removes the need to use authority to manufacture personal security.

The leader can therefore focus on faithful participation rather than personal validation.

And this reveals something larger about relationships themselves.

Relationships Are Places Where Christ Is Revealed

In Chapter Four, we established that our assignments are also places where Christ can be revealed and where the ministry of reconciliation can be expressed.

Relationships make that visible in very practical ways.

People encounter something about Christ through how believers treat them.

Through patience.

Truth.

Mercy. Integrity.

Service.

Forgiveness.

Generosity.

Correction given without contempt.

Leadership exercised without oppression.

Strength that does not need to humiliate.

Love that does not demand repayment before it can remain love.

This does not mean every interaction must become a sermon.

It means Christ is revealed through the character with which we carry our responsibilities.

Jesus said that people should see our good works and glorify our Father in heaven.

So relational faithfulness is not merely about maintaining peaceful relationships.

It is also part of our witness.

We represent Christ among people.

And that gives us another reason not to make relationships purely transactional. If I only love those who respond correctly, my love is governed by their performance.

But grace has taught me another pattern.

God acted towards me from grace.

I received.

Now grace shapes how I participate with others.

This does not guarantee that every relationship will produce the outcome I desire.

But it does mean that another person's poor response does not have to determine the character of mine.

When Relationships Become Another Performance System

We can now recognise the pattern.

Performance says:

If I love well, you owe me the response I want.

Grace says:

I will love faithfully without turning love into currency.

Performance says: Your disappointment proves my failure.

Grace says:

I will honestly examine my responsibility without assuming control over what belongs to you.

Performance says:

I must control your choices.

Grace says:

I can influence, teach, serve, correct and speak truth, but I am not sovereign over another person's heart.

Performance says:

If you do not appreciate me, my service was worthless.

Grace says:

My service can remain meaningful before God even when recognition is limited.

Performance says:

If I forgive you, I must pretend nothing happened.

Grace says:

I can forgive while continuing to walk in truth and wisdom. Performance says:

If I establish a boundary, I am no longer loving.

Grace says:

Love and wisdom do not have to oppose each other.

The psychological shift is profound.

I no longer have to make another person's response the measure of my identity.

I can take responsibility without carrying false responsibility.

I can apologise without controlling the response.

I can serve without purchasing recognition.

I can love without making love a debt.

I can forgive without abandoning wisdom.

I can influence without trying to become sovereign.

And because I am secure enough to do this, I can also evaluate relationships honestly.

Working the Grace Cycle in Relationships

The pattern we have used throughout this book can now be applied to relationships.

Receive.

Begin with what grace has already given.

Receive God's love.

Receive your identity in Christ.

Receive wisdom from His Word.

Recognise the responsibilities presently entrusted to you.

You are not entering the relationship empty, demanding that another person fill what only God can supply.

Believe.

Believe what God says about love, truth, forgiveness, responsibility and your identity.

Do not allow another person's approval or rejection to become the final authority over who you are.

Expect.

Expect grace to produce fruit. Expect healthy relationships to grow where there is mutual participation.

Expect children to learn.

Expect communication to improve when people genuinely work at it.

Expect reconciliation where there is a sound basis for it.

But do not confuse expectation with control.

Faith can expect while still recognising another person's responsibility.

Act.

Love.

Listen.

Speak truth.

Serve.

Apologise.

Correct.

Encourage.

Pray. Forgive.

Establish boundaries where necessary.

Fulfil your responsibilities.

Evaluate.

What is actually happening?

Is communication improving?

Is trust increasing?

Are the same harmful patterns continuing?

Is your own behaviour contributing to the problem?

Are you listening?

Are your expectations reasonable?

Is the other person taking responsibility?

Evaluation is not condemnation. It is information.

Learn.

Perhaps you need greater patience.

Perhaps clearer communication.

Perhaps stronger boundaries. Perhaps an apology.

Perhaps outside help.

Perhaps you need to stop trying to control something that does not belong to you.

Perhaps you need to become more faithful in something that does.

Return to Grace.

When disappointment comes, do not immediately turn it into condemnation.

Do not rebuild performance religion inside the relationship.

Return to what God has supplied.

Remember who you are.

Remember what belongs to you and what belongs to God.

Then act again.

Continue where continued participation is wise.

Adjust where adjustment is necessary.

Rebuild where rebuilding is possible.

Create appropriate distance where wisdom requires it. Forgive where forgiveness is needed.

Remain truthful.

Remain teachable.

Remain grounded in grace.

This is not passive love.

It is love free from the exhausting burden of trying to become God over another person's heart.

Love Without Becoming God

There is deep humility in working from grace in relationships.

We do our part.

And we take our part seriously.

We love.

We serve.

We listen.

We teach.

We provide.

We correct. We apologise.

We forgive.

We protect.

We establish boundaries.

We pray.

We remain open to correction ourselves.

We work towards healthy outcomes.

But we refuse to carry a responsibility God never gave us.

We cannot control every heart.

We cannot guarantee every response.

We cannot make every person appreciate us.

We cannot force every child to make the right choice.

We cannot make every broken relationship reconcile merely because we desire reconciliation.

We cannot manufacture another person's repentance.

We can participate faithfully.

And then we trust God.

I am responsible for loving, serving, speaking truth, forgiving, correcting, apologising and acting faithfully. I will examine myself honestly and change where I need to change. I will not use grace as an excuse for irresponsibility. But neither will I assume responsibility for becoming the controller of another person's heart. I can influence without becoming sovereign. I can love without turning love into currency. I can serve without making recognition my reward. I can forgive without abandoning wisdom. I can establish boundaries without hatred. Another person's response does not determine my identity, and their disappointment does not automatically prove my failure. Grace frees me to do my part faithfully while trusting God with what lies beyond my control.

This makes love more honest.

Service becomes freer.

Correction becomes humbler.

Leadership becomes less insecure. Boundaries become less hostile.

Apologies become more sincere.

Forgiveness becomes less confused.

And people can remain deeply precious without being required to become our source.

That is what working from grace begins to look like when grace enters our homes, friendships, workplaces, churches and communities.

Yet even when we understand our assignment, trust God as Source, and learn to relate to people without trying to control them, another difficulty remains.

Sometimes we participate faithfully and the result still does not come when we expected.

We believe.

We work.

We steward.

We love.

We continue.

And still we wait. Then the question is no longer simply how to work from grace when resources are limited or relationships are difficult.

It becomes:

How do we continue working from grace when the outcome is delayed, circumstances change, setbacks occur, and we cannot yet see how the story will unfold?

That is where we turn next.
