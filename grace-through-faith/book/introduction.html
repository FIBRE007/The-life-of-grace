Introduction

What If We Have Been Working for What Grace Has Already Provided?

Imagine that you desperately need something.

Perhaps it is provision for your family.

Perhaps you need wisdom for a decision, resources for a business, strength for a responsibility, direction concerning your future, help with an assignment, or a breakthrough in circumstances that have resisted everything you know to do.

You believe God can help you.

You have read His Word. You have heard testimonies. You know stories of people in Scripture who depended upon Him and experienced His intervention.

But before you approach what God has provided, you begin examining yourself. Have I done enough?

Have I prayed enough?

Is my faith strong enough?

Have I given enough?

Have I been righteous enough?

Did I doubt somewhere along the way?

Is there something else I must do before God will help me?

Something very important has happened.

The focus has moved from the sufficiency of God to the sufficiency of your performance.

The Equation We Understand

Performance and reward are familiar to us.

A worker performs a job and receives wages.

A pupil studies and earns a grade.

An athlete performs and receives recognition.

A business creates value and earns income.

In their proper places, these systems are useful. But problems begin when we assume that grace operates according to exactly the same equation:

Performance→Righteousness→God's Approval→Blessing

Under this system, blessing becomes evidence that we have performed correctly.

Difficulty then creates a frightening question:

What did I do wrong?

Success can produce pride.

Failure can produce condemnation.

Another person's blessing can create comparison.

Delay can create anxiety.

And uncertainty about our performance can produce uncertainty about God's willingness to help us.

Christianity announces something radically different.

We are saved by grace through faith, not by works.

But it is possible to accept that statement theologically while continuing to live psychologically according to performance. We simply replace one kind of work with another.

When Faith Becomes Another Work

The old equation says:

Good Works→Righteousness→God's Approval→Blessing

The Christian version can quietly become:

Faith Performance→God's Approval→Blessing

Now we are not trying to prove that we have performed enough good works.

We are trying to prove that we have enough faith.

We pray more.

We carefully monitor our words.

We try not to acknowledge uncertainty.

We become frightened by doubtful thoughts.

We give.

We serve. We confess.

None of these things is inherently wrong. Prayer, giving, service and confession all have important places in Christian living.

The question is why we are doing them.

Have we turned faith—the means through which grace is received—into another work through which grace must be earned?

That question deserves serious examination.

Reward or Resource?

Consider an important distinction.

A reward is given because something has been accomplished.

A resource is provided because something needs to be accomplished.

If I employ someone to build something, it would make little sense to withhold every tool required for the assignment and promise to hand over the tools only after the work has been completed.

The tools are not the worker's reward.

They are part of what makes the work possible. What if we have misunderstood some of the blessings of God because we have treated resources as rewards?

Life itself is a gift.

Wisdom is necessary for living.

Strength is necessary for work.

Relationships matter. Opportunity matters. Provision matters. Direction matters. Knowledge matters. Grace matters.

What if we are exhausting ourselves trying to earn the very resources God intended us to use for the journey?

This does not mean Scripture teaches nothing about reward. It certainly does.

But reward, grace and provision are not identical concepts. We will need to distinguish them carefully.

What About Eternal Life?

Perhaps nowhere is this distinction more important than in our understanding of eternal life.

Christians often speak of eternal life primarily as something waiting for us after death. Yet Jesus speaks of the person who believes as having eternal life (John 5:24). 1 John likewise speaks of believers knowing that they have eternal life (1 John 5:13).

Jesus' words in John 17:3 connect eternal life with knowing the Father and Jesus Christ.

Romans 6:23 makes another remarkable distinction. Sin pays wages, while eternal life is described as God's gift.

This suggests something worth investigating.

What if eternal life is not merely God's reward at the end of a successfully completed life of faith?

What if it is the life we receive through Christ—the life from which the Christian journey is meant to be lived, beginning now and continuing into eternity?

If that is true, we are not trying to reach the end of the journey in order to earn life.

We are travelling because we have received life.

That changes the psychology of the journey.

Was Grace Present Under the Law?

Another assumption must also be examined. Did grace begin in the New Testament?

Consider King Jehoshaphat.

2 Chronicles 20 describes Judah confronting a threat beyond its ability to overcome. Jehoshaphat did not pretend the problem was small. He sought God.

God responded through His prophet.

Then came Jehoshaphat's remarkable instruction:

Believe in the Lord your God; believe His prophets

2 Chronicles 20:20

Look at the order.

There was need. God provided a word. The people believed. Their belief affected their actions. And they experienced what God had revealed.

This raises an important question:

❦

Did their faith make God willing to help them, or did their faith enable them to participate in what God's grace had revealed?

If the second is true, then grace and faith were already visible even during the dispensation of law.

And Jehoshaphat is not our only exhibit.

Abraham believed. Noah believed and built. Moses depended upon God for what human strength could not accomplish. Israel ate manna it could not manufacture. David repeatedly depended upon a strength beyond his own.

The Old Testament may have far more to teach us about grace than we sometimes recognise.

But Those People Were Special

There is another way we can distance ourselves from biblical testimony.

We say:

"That was Abraham."

"That was Moses."

"That was David."

"That was Elijah."

"That was Paul."

Perhaps without saying it aloud, we conclude: "They were special."

But Scripture repeatedly confronts the idea of divine partiality. God does not operate through the kind of personal favouritism human beings practise (Romans 2:11; Acts 10:34).

This should affect the way we read biblical testimony.

If we treat biblical characters merely as exceptional people who received exceptional treatment from God, their stories may produce admiration without expectation.

We admire Abraham's faith. We admire David's victories. We admire Jehoshaphat's deliverance. We admire Paul's ministry.

But we never ask the more challenging question:

❦

What does their experience reveal about the God upon whom I also depend?

The Promises Find Their Yes in Christ

This question becomes even more important when we encounter Paul's statement in 2 Corinthians 1:20 that the promises of God find their Yes in Christ. What does that mean?

How should someone who is in Christ read the promises of Scripture?

Should every promise made to Abraham, Moses, Joshua or David simply be dismissed with the words, "That was for them"?

Or do those promises reveal the grace, character and provision of God that reaches its fulfilment in Christ?

There are important interpretive questions here.

A promise concerning David's throne, for example, cannot simply be treated in exactly the same way as a promise of God's help. God's promise concerning Israel's possession of Canaan cannot carelessly be transformed into a promise that every Christian will acquire whatever property they desire.

Faith cannot mean taking whatever we want, finding a biblical story that resembles it, and declaring that God must reproduce the same circumstances.

That would make our desires the source of the promise.

Biblical faith must begin somewhere else:

What has God revealed? Yet if God has no favourites, and if His promises find their Yes in Christ, then we must also resist the opposite error of reading Scripture as though God's faithfulness belongs exclusively to people who lived thousands of years ago.

This book will explore that tension.

Not to weaken expectation.

To give expectation a biblical foundation.

Grace Produces Expectation

Grace is not resignation.

Faith does not say:

"I believe God, therefore I expect nothing."

When God reveals something, faith believes Him.

And belief naturally creates expectation.

The important distinction is between outcome, process and timing.

God may reveal what gives us grounds to expect an outcome without telling us everything about the process.

Abraham had a promise, but the timing tested him. Joseph had dreams from God, but the process appeared for years to move in the opposite direction.

Jehoshaphat received God's word concerning the battle, but he still had to walk towards the situation he feared.

And the supreme revelation is Christ Himself.

The cross appeared to contradict victory. Death appeared to contradict life. The grave appeared to contradict everything His followers had hoped.

Yet the resurrection revealed that what looked like contradiction was not the conclusion.

Present circumstances had not been given the final word.

Promise Is Not Process

This distinction will become important throughout this book:

Promise is not process.

Outcome is not timing.

Present circumstances are not necessarily the final interpretation of God's faithfulness.

Sometimes we receive a promise and then unconsciously invent the process. We decide how it should happen. We decide when it should happen. We decide who should be involved.

Then, when the process does not follow our expectations, we assume the promise itself has failed.

What if it has not?

What if the question we sometimes need to ask is:

❦

Has God's promise changed, or has the process simply failed to follow the route I expected?

This is where grace can produce extraordinary resilience.

Grace and the Courage to Work

What happens when failure of a particular attempt no longer means God has rejected you?

You can learn.

What happens when correction no longer means you are worthless?

You can listen. What happens when another person's success no longer means God's grace has run out?

You can celebrate them and continue working.

What happens when a failed strategy does not automatically mean the expected outcome has disappeared?

You can change strategy.

What happens when delay does not automatically mean God's Word has failed?

You can wait without becoming passive.

You can prepare. You can work. You can develop competence. You can ask questions. You can make adjustments. You can try again.

This is where the psychology of grace becomes intensely practical.

The pattern we will eventually explore is:

Receive→Believe→Expect→Act→Evaluate→Learn→Return to Grace→Act Again

Grace does not terminate action. Grace becomes its foundation.

The Question Before Us

So this book begins with a question.

Are we working for grace, or are we working from grace?

What if faith was never intended to persuade God to become gracious?

What if God's grace is what gives faith something to receive?

What if some of the things we have been desperately trying to earn are resources God knows we need in order to live the life He has called us to live?

What would happen to our courage? What would happen to our work? What would happen to our expectations? What would happen when we encounter setbacks? What would happen when the process takes longer than expected?

Perhaps grace does not call us to labour less.

Perhaps grace finally frees us to labour without making our performance the basis upon which we expect God to be faithful.

And if we are going to understand that freedom, we must first confront the system that keeps pulling us in the opposite direction. We must begin with the performance problem.
