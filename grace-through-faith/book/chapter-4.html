Chapter Four

Working From Grace in Purpose, Assignment, Work and Ministry

Grace gives us a reason to labour. We do not work to persuade God to become involved in our lives; we work because we believe He is involved. We plant, we water, we prepare, we learn and we apply ourselves, while recognising that God remains the Source of the increase.

But once we understand this, another question becomes unavoidable: What are we giving all this labour to?

It is possible to become very good at working without ever becoming clear about what our work is serving. We can become busy, productive and disciplined and still need to answer a more fundamental question: What is my life for?

For the believer, that question reaches beyond career, achievement or personal ambition. We received eternal life when we received Christ, yet we remain in this world. There are days still to live, relationships still to encounter, responsibilities still to carry and work still to do. Those remaining days are not simply a waiting room between salvation and heaven. They are part of the life we have been given, and they are meant to produce something that ultimately points back to God.

Jesus gives us a simple picture of this in Matthew 5:16:

"Let your light so shine before men, that they may see your good works, and glorify your Father which is in heaven."

Notice where the movement ends: with the Father receiving glory.

People see something produced through our lives, but the final attention is meant to move beyond us to God. This gives us a useful way to understand purpose.

Our fundamental purpose is not simply to find a profession that makes us feel fulfilled. It is not to become a teacher, doctor, entrepreneur, pastor, artist, parent or leader. These may become important expressions of our lives, but none of them is large enough to define our ultimate purpose.

Our purpose is to glorify God—to live in such a way that who He is becomes visible as deserving glory, honour and power.

That purpose is large enough to remain when our circumstances change. But because it is so large, we need to understand how it enters the ordinary details of life. How does glorifying God become practical when someone wakes up on Monday morning and has children to prepare for school, customers to serve, an examination to study for, a business to build or employment to search for?

This is where assignment becomes important.

Purpose Becomes Practical Through Assignment

Purpose and assignment are related, but they are not the same thing.

Purpose answers the larger question:

Why does my life ultimately exist?

Assignment asks a more immediate question:

What has been placed before me to do now?

Assignments are the responsibilities, tasks and good works that occupy particular seasons of our lives.

For one person, a present assignment may include building a business. For another, it may include studying. Someone else may be raising children while managing a career. An unemployed person's immediate assignment may include searching diligently for profitable work, developing useful skills and responsibly managing what is presently available.

Even responsibilities that appear ordinary matter.

Being a good parent matters.

Being a responsible child matters.

Being a good student matters.

Being a trustworthy employee matters.

Being a helpful neighbour matters.

Responding with love to a stranger matters.

An assignment does not have to become famous before it becomes meaningful.

This distinction between purpose and assignment becomes especially important because assignments change.

A student eventually leaves school. Children grow. Jobs change. Projects finish. Businesses develop through different stages. Leadership responsibilities may increase and later decrease. A person may carry many responsibilities in one season and relatively few in another. If we confuse assignment with purpose, every major change in responsibility can become a crisis of identity.

A person loses a job and thinks, I have lost my purpose.

A parent reaches a new stage of life after the children become independent and wonders, What is left for me now?

A project ends and the person who built their identity around it feels empty.

But an assignment can end without purpose ending.

The form of our responsibility may change while the larger reason for our lives remains: God is still to be glorified through us.

This means the question that serves us better through changing seasons is not merely, What is my great calling?

It is:

What has been entrusted to me now?

That question brings purpose into the present.

Life Is Time Given for Assignment

Assignments take place within something every living person is continually spending: time. Psalm 90:12 says:

"So teach us to number our days, that we may apply our hearts unto wisdom."

Paul similarly urges believers in Ephesians 5:15–16 to walk wisely, "redeeming the time."

Our lives in this world can therefore be understood, in part, as time entrusted to us in which responsibilities can be fulfilled.

This does not mean every hour must be filled with visible productivity. Rest has its place. Recovery has its place. Relationships have their place. Quiet seasons have their place. The point is not frantic activity. It is conscious stewardship.

Some seasons are peak periods.

Responsibilities multiply. Children need attention. Work becomes demanding. Opportunities increase. People depend on us. Several assignments may exist at the same time.

Other seasons are quieter.

Some responsibilities end. Capacity may temporarily reduce. A previous assignment may have been completed while the next is not yet clear.

Neither season determines our worth. The believer remains attentive to the responsibilities of the present season rather than measuring significance merely by how busy life feels.

And once we recognise what has been entrusted to us, Chapter Three's teaching about labour becomes very practical. We now know where to direct our participation.

Grace-centred labour is not merely about being busy. It is about applying ourselves faithfully towards what has been entrusted to us.

That means assignment should affect preparation.

We Prepare Because the Assignment Matters

Joseph's story gives us a powerful picture of development through process. The young man who received dreams was not immediately placed over Egypt. Between the dream and the eventual responsibility were years in which he learned to manage, serve and exercise responsibility under difficult circumstances.

The process did not make God's involvement less real. It developed Joseph for responsibilities that would eventually require far more than enthusiasm. We see another expression of this in Nehemiah. He prayed when he heard about the condition of Jerusalem, but prayer did not make planning unnecessary. When opportunity came before the king, Nehemiah could explain what he needed. Later he examined Jerusalem's walls, organised people and responded strategically to opposition.

Bezalel gives us yet another picture. Exodus 31 describes him as filled by God with wisdom, understanding, knowledge and skill for the work of the tabernacle. Divine enablement and skilled workmanship appear together.

These examples show why preparation should not be treated as evidence that we are depending less upon God.

If an assignment requires knowledge, learn.

If it requires skill, develop skill.

If it requires planning, plan.

If it requires collaboration, learn to work with people.

If the assignment must begin small, begin with what is presently possible.

Preparation can itself become an expression of expectation. We prepare because we believe there is something worth preparing for.

This also means that we should not become so attached to our first method that we confuse it with the assignment itself.

A person searching for employment may need to change how they search.

A business owner may need to change an offer.

A student may need to change a study method.

A leader may need to learn a better way to communicate.

The assignment may remain while the method changes.

Rejection can therefore become information without becoming a verdict on the future. A closed route may tell us something about that route without telling us everything about the outcome we expect.

The believer can remain confident in grace while remaining teachable about participation.

Yet there is another dimension of assignment that goes beyond completing tasks successfully. If our ultimate purpose is God's glory, then how we fulfil an assignment matters alongside what we accomplish through it.

That brings us back to the words of Jesus: "that they may see your good works."

The Place of Assignment Is a Place Where Christ Becomes Visible

Our assignments place our lives where other people can observe them.

A customer experiences the way a Christian business owner conducts business. A pupil experiences the way a Christian teacher teaches. A colleague experiences the character of a Christian employee. Children experience Christianity partly through the way Christian parents live with them.

This means our work communicates something before we deliberately say anything.

Integrity communicates.

Diligence communicates.

Patience communicates.

Compassion communicates.

Responsibility communicates.

Excellence communicates.

The believer does not practise these things in order to earn a relationship with God. That would return us to working for grace. Instead, they should increasingly become the visible fruit of a life already touched by grace.

We do not perform good works to become God's children. Because we belong to Him, something of His influence should become visible in the way we carry our responsibilities.

That is why quality matters.

When Jesus speaks about light shining before people, the destination is not admiration of the believer. He says they should see our good works and glorify our Father.

Our work can therefore become a window.

People see the work, but something beyond the worker becomes visible through it.

Yet this raises another important question. If a believer works with integrity, serves people well, behaves compassionately and produces excellent results, has that believer completely fulfilled their responsibility towards the people around them?

Not necessarily.

Because good works are not the same thing as the ministry of reconciliation.

Where Good Works Meet the Ministry of Reconciliation

Paul writes in 2 Corinthians 5:18 that God "hath reconciled us to himself by Jesus Christ, and hath given to us the ministry of reconciliation."

He continues by describing believers as ambassadors for Christ.

This adds another dimension to our lives in the world.

The God who reconciled us to Himself has entrusted to us a message through which others can be called to reconciliation with Him.

That is different from good works, even though the two should support each other.

Good works make the influence of Christ visible. The ministry of reconciliation makes the message of Christ known.

A person can observe that you are honest without knowing why Christ died.

Someone can admire your generosity without understanding reconciliation with God. A colleague may respect your discipline without knowing the gospel.

Good works can make people ask questions. They can give credibility to our witness. They can demonstrate something of the transformation grace produces. But good behaviour by itself does not fully communicate who Christ is and what He has done.

Christ must also be made known.

At the same time, our spoken witness should not contradict our visible lives. It is difficult to speak persuasively about Christ while repeatedly treating people dishonestly, carelessly or harshly.

The two therefore meet without becoming identical.

Our lives should allow people to see something of Christ's influence, while our ministry of reconciliation gives people opportunity to hear the message of Christ.

And once we understand this, something significant becomes visible about assignment.

The place where we carry out our assignments is often the same place where we meet the people among whom we can fulfil the ministry of reconciliation.

Your Assignment Places You Among People

A teacher's assignment places that teacher among pupils, colleagues and parents.

A business owner encounters customers, employees, suppliers and other businesspeople.

A student encounters classmates and teachers.

An employee encounters colleagues, supervisors and clients.

A parent lives among children, relatives, neighbours and other families.

Even someone searching for employment is interacting with people while carrying out that assignment.

These people are not merely parts of the machinery through which our assignments succeed.

They are people God loves.

This changes the way we understand ministry.

We do not necessarily have to leave ordinary life before ministry begins.

The office can become a place where ministry happens.

The classroom can become a place where ministry happens. The marketplace can become a place where ministry happens.

The home and neighbourhood can become places where ministry happens.

This does not mean the occupation itself becomes identical to the ministry of reconciliation. Teaching mathematics is still teaching mathematics. Managing accounts is still managing accounts. Running a business is still running a business.

But the believer carrying out that assignment remains an ambassador for Christ while doing it.

The assignment gives us responsibilities to perform, but it also creates a human environment in which Christ can be revealed through our lives and, as appropriate opportunities arise, proclaimed through our words.

That requires wisdom.

People should not be treated merely as evangelistic projects. Christian witness should not become manipulation or forced religious conversation. Love listens. Wisdom recognises appropriate opportunities. Respect recognises the dignity of the person before us.

But the believer should remain conscious:

Wherever my assignment places me among people, I have not stopped being Christ's ambassador.

This means ministry does not begin only when we enter a church building, stand behind a pulpit or join an organised outreach.

We carry Christ into ordinary life.

And this brings the chapter's ideas together in a way that makes even ordinary assignments deeply significant.

The Result Must Point Beyond Us

Assignments should produce results.

A teacher should desire students to learn.

A student should desire understanding and progress.

A business should create value and seek sustainability.

A parent should desire the healthy development of the child.

An employee should produce useful work.

A person searching for employment should desire profitable employment.

Caring about results is not opposed to grace. But the result is not the final destination.

Remember Jesus' words:

"…that they may see your good works, and glorify your Father which is in heaven."

We participate towards outcomes, but the fruit of our lives should ultimately point beyond us.

This protects us from making assignment our identity.

A successful business can glorify God, but the business is not God.

A career can become an important assignment, but it is not our ultimate purpose.

A ministry responsibility can be meaningful, but even that assignment does not become our identity.

Assignments can change.

Seasons can change.

Methods can change.

Responsibilities can increase and decrease.

But the believer continues to ask:

What has been entrusted to me now, and how can I faithfully participate in it so that God is glorified?

That question brings purpose out of the abstract and places it inside ordinary life.

What responsibilities are before me?

Who is affected by the way I carry them?

What skills must I develop?

What good should this work produce?

What does my conduct reveal about Christ?

Who am I encountering because of this assignment?

Where might I have an opportunity to carry the message of reconciliation?

And when the result comes, who will receive the glory?

These questions reveal the larger movement:

Purpose gives life direction. Assignment gives purpose present expression. Labour is our participation within the assignment. Good works allow the influence of Christ to become visible. The ministry of reconciliation allows Christ's message to become known. Results fulfil responsibility and serve people. And through it all, God is meant to receive the glory.

Our lives, then, are not empty time between salvation and eternity.

They are time entrusted to us.

There is something before us now.

There are responsibilities now.

There are people now.

There is good to do now.

There is Christ to reveal now.

There is reconciliation to minister now.

And there is a God worthy of being glorified through it all.

My purpose is greater than my profession, and my assignment is greater than a title. My life is time entrusted to me for the responsibilities placed before me. Therefore, I will not despise an assignment because it appears ordinary. I will prepare. I will learn. I will serve. I will work faithfully with what has been entrusted to me. I will allow the influence of Christ to become visible through my good works, while remembering that good works do not replace the ministry of reconciliation. Wherever my assignment places me among people, I remain an ambassador for Christ. And as my life and labour produce fruit, I want that fruit to point beyond me to the God who deserves the glory.

But recognising an assignment creates another practical question.

Every assignment requires something.

Work requires strength. Decisions require wisdom. Businesses require resources. Families require provision. Learning requires opportunity. Labour requires health and capacity. Assignments may require relationships, money, knowledge, tools, access and time. If God has given us life and responsibility, what should we believe about the resources required to carry them out?

That question brings us to another important dimension of working from grace:

God as our Source and Supplier.
